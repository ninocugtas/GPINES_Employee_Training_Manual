## Why Resident Rights Matter

### The Foundation of Person-Centered Care

**Resident rights are not privileges—they are legal protections guaranteed by Michigan law.** Even when someone needs assistance with daily activities or has cognitive impairment, they remain a person with dignity, autonomy, and legal rights.

**Historical context:**
Before resident rights laws, people in institutions often experienced:
- Loss of personal freedom and choice
- Lack of privacy and dignity
- Inability to voice complaints or concerns
- Control over all aspects of their lives by staff
- Treatment as patients or "cases" rather than individuals

The resident rights movement emerged from advocacy efforts to protect vulnerable adults from abuse, neglect, and dehumanizing treatment. Today, these rights are enforceable by law.

### Legal Authority

**Michigan Administrative Code R 400.14304 - Resident Rights**
This regulation specifies 17 distinct rights that EVERY resident in an Adult Foster Care facility must be afforded. Violations of these rights can result in:
- State licensing citations
- Fines and penalties
- Corrective action plans
- License suspension or revocation
- Legal liability and lawsuits
- Criminal charges (in cases of abuse or exploitation)

**Federal protections also apply:**
- Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)
- Fair Housing Act
- Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)
- Older Americans Act
- Civil Rights protections

### Your Role in Protecting Resident Rights

As a direct care worker, you are on the front lines of resident rights protection. You interact with residents more than anyone else in the facility. **You have both the responsibility and the opportunity to ensure residents' rights are respected every single day.**

**Key responsibilities:**
- Know and understand all resident rights
- Actively protect and promote these rights in daily care
- Recognize when rights are being violated (by yourself or others)
- Advocate for residents who cannot advocate for themselves
- Report rights violations to your supervisor
- Support residents in exercising their rights

## The 17 Resident Rights Under Michigan Law

Michigan Administrative Code R 400.14304 guarantees the following rights. We'll explore each one in detail.

### Right 1: To Be Treated with Dignity and Respect

**What it means:**
Every resident has the right to be treated as a valued individual worthy of respect, regardless of their physical or cognitive abilities.

**In practice:**
- Address residents by their preferred name (not "honey," "sweetie," unless they prefer it)
- Knock before entering their room
- Speak TO residents, not about them as if they're not there
- Respect their opinions and preferences, even if you disagree
- Avoid infantilizing language or tone (don't talk to adults like children)
- Honor their life experience and wisdom

**Violation examples:**
- Discussing a resident's incontinence in front of others
- Calling an adult "a good girl" when they finish eating
- Rolling your eyes or making dismissive comments about a resident's requests
- Ignoring a resident when they're speaking to you

**Real-world scenario:**
Mrs. Anderson insists on wearing her favorite pink sweater every day, even though it has a small stain. Instead of forcing her to change ("You can't wear that, it's dirty"), respect her choice and offer to help clean it: "Mrs. Anderson, I see you love that pink sweater. How about I help you wash it tonight so you can wear it fresh tomorrow?"

### Right 2: To Be Free from Abuse and Neglect

**What it means:**
Residents have the absolute right to be protected from physical, emotional, sexual, or financial abuse, and from neglect of their care needs.

**Types of abuse covered:**
- **Physical abuse:** Hitting, slapping, rough handling, inappropriate restraint
- **Emotional/verbal abuse:** Yelling, threatening, humiliation, intimidation
- **Sexual abuse:** Any unwanted sexual contact or behavior
- **Financial exploitation:** Stealing money or belongings, coercing financial transactions
- **Neglect:** Failure to provide necessary care, food, water, medication, supervision

**In practice:**
- Provide gentle, respectful care
- Never use physical force except in immediate danger situations
- Report suspected abuse by anyone (staff, other residents, family, visitors)
- Ensure all care needs are met promptly
- Protect residents' belongings and finances

**If you witness abuse:**
- Intervene if safe to do so (stop the abusive behavior)
- Report immediately to your supervisor
- File a report with Adult Protective Services (855-444-3911) within 24 hours
- Document what you witnessed objectively

### Right 3: To Privacy and Confidentiality

**What it means:**
Residents have the right to personal privacy in their living space, personal care, communications, and medical information.

**In practice:**
- Close doors and draw curtains during personal care
- Knock and wait for permission before entering private rooms
- Don't share resident information with unauthorized people
- Respect private phone calls and mail
- Don't discuss residents in public areas or with other residents
- Follow HIPAA requirements (covered in Module 7)

**Violation examples:**
- Leaving bathroom door open while assisting with toileting
- Discussing Mrs. Johnson's medication list in the dining room
- Reading a resident's mail or listening to phone conversations
- Entering a resident's room without knocking
- Posting about residents on social media (even without names)

**Balancing privacy with safety:**
Sometimes safety requires some limitation on complete privacy (checking on residents, door alarms for wandering risk). These should be:
- Documented in the service plan
- The least restrictive method possible
- Explained to the resident and family
- Regularly reviewed for continued necessity

### Right 4: To Control Their Own Finances

**What it means:**
Residents have the right to manage their own money, access their funds, and make financial decisions.

**In practice:**
- Residents can keep money in their rooms (though facility may recommend safe storage)
- If facility manages resident funds, detailed accounting required
- Residents must have access to their money when requested
- No one can force a resident to pay for additional services
- Residents can make purchases, give gifts, or spend as they choose

**Your role:**
- NEVER borrow money from residents
- NEVER accept expensive gifts (small tokens like cookies are usually okay—check facility policy)
- Report if you see anyone taking resident money or belongings
- Don't pressure residents to buy or pay for anything
- If a resident asks you to help with finances, involve your supervisor

**Red flags for financial exploitation:**
- Resident suddenly can't afford things they previously could
- Unexplained withdrawals or purchases
- New "friends" or family members suddenly very interested in resident's finances
- Resident seems confused or coerced about financial decisions
- Missing personal items or cash

### Right 5: To Privacy in Communications

**What it means:**
Residents can send and receive mail, make phone calls, and have visitors without interference or monitoring.

**In practice:**
- Don't open residents' mail (unless they ask for help)
- Provide access to telephone in private
- Don't listen to phone conversations
- Allow visitors during reasonable hours
- Don't screen visitors unless there's documented safety concern

**Exceptions:**
- Court orders (restraining orders against specific visitors)
- Documented safety concerns in service plan
- Visitor behavior that threatens resident or others

**Real-world scenario:**
Mr. Davis's daughter calls and asks you not to tell him she called because "it upsets him." You should explain that you cannot withhold this information from him—it's his right to know who called. The daughter can choose not to call if she doesn't want him to know.

### Right 6: To Have and Use Personal Possessions

**What it means:**
Residents can bring and keep personal belongings, furniture, and items that make their space feel like home (within space and safety limitations).

**In practice:**
- Allow personal furniture, photos, decorations in resident rooms
- Respect residents' belongings—don't move or remove without permission
- Don't borrow or use residents' items
- Help residents keep track of personal items
- Maintain property inventory

**Reasonable limitations:**
- No items that pose fire hazards (candles, incense, certain appliances)
- No weapons
- Space limitations in shared rooms
- Items that pose safety risks to the resident or others

**If items must be restricted:**
- Explain the safety reason clearly
- Offer alternatives when possible
- Document in service plan
- Store items safely if resident wants to keep them but can't have in room

### Right 7: To Receive Visitors

**What it means:**
Residents can have visitors of their choosing at reasonable times. They can also refuse to see visitors.

**In practice:**
- Welcome family and friends during visiting hours
- Extend visiting hours for out-of-town family when reasonable
- Don't restrict visitors unless documented safety reason
- Respect when residents don't want to see certain people
- Provide private space for visits when possible

**The resident decides:**
- Who they want to see
- When they want visits (within facility hours)
- Whether to see someone who shows up
- How long visits last

**Balancing rights:**
If a visitor is disruptive, intoxicated, or threatening, you can ask them to leave. But the DEFAULT is that visitors are welcomed.

**Dementia considerations:**
Even if a resident doesn't remember the person visiting, visits can still be meaningful. Don't restrict visits just because the resident "won't remember." However, if visits cause significant distress, discuss with family and supervisor.

### Right 8: To Leave the Facility

**What it means:**
Residents can leave the facility for appointments, outings, visits, or to move to another residence. They are not prisoners.

**In practice:**
- Residents can go for walks, shopping, appointments
- Can visit family or go on outings
- Can choose to move to a different facility
- Can go outside for fresh air

**Important distinctions:**
- **Voluntary residents:** Can leave anytime (though facility may have sign-out procedures for safety)
- **Involuntary commitment:** Very rare in AFC; requires court order for mental health treatment

**Safety considerations:**
- For residents with dementia at risk of wandering, service plan may include supervision or door alarms
- These must be LEAST restrictive necessary
- Cannot "lock in" residents without legal authority
- Must balance safety with freedom

**Scenario:**
Mrs. Chen wants to walk to the corner store. She's able to walk safely and knows the area. Even if it's more convenient for you to drive her, it's her right to walk independently if she chooses.

### Right 9: To Participate in Activities

**What it means:**
Residents have the right to participate in social, religious, and community activities of their choice.

**In practice:**
- Offer regular activities (crafts, games, music, outings)
- Support residents attending religious services
- Facilitate community involvement (voting, clubs, volunteer work)
- Respect cultural and religious practices
- Accommodate individual interests

**Also the right NOT to participate:**
- Residents can decline activities
- Don't force participation
- Offer choices, not mandates
- Respect different activity preferences

**Your role:**
- Encourage participation without pressure
- Provide opportunities for meaningful activities
- Learn residents' interests and incorporate them
- Make activities accessible (adaptive equipment, assistance as needed)

### Right 10: To Be Free from Physical and Chemical Restraints

**What it means:**
Residents cannot be restrained unless there's immediate danger and no other way to protect them or others.

**Physical restraints:**
- Anything that restricts movement: vests, belts, side rails used to prevent getting up, locked chairs
- Holding or blocking a resident from moving

**Chemical restraints:**
- Medications given primarily to control behavior rather than treat a medical condition
- "Snowing" residents with sedatives to make them easier to manage

**When restraints MAY be used:**
- ONLY in emergency situations where person is immediate danger to self or others
- ONLY until the danger passes
- MUST be documented with justification
- MUST try less restrictive interventions first
- MUST have physician order (except emergencies)
- MUST be monitored constantly

**Less restrictive alternatives:**
- Behavior interventions (validation, redirection, environmental changes)
- Supervised activities to prevent wandering
- Addressing underlying needs (pain, hunger, boredom, toileting)
- Adaptive equipment (low beds, cushioned floors, grab bars)

**Important:** Side rails on beds CAN be restraints if used to prevent a resident from getting up. They're only acceptable if used for positioning or resident requests them.

### Right 11: To Self-Administer Medication

**What it means:**
If a resident is capable, they can manage and take their own medications without staff administration.

**In practice:**
- Assess resident's ability to self-administer safely
- Provide locked storage if resident managing own medications
- Monitor for safety but don't control unless necessary
- Re-evaluate if abilities change

**When self-administration is appropriate:**
- Resident has cognitive ability to remember doses and times
- Resident has physical ability to open bottles, read labels
- Resident wants to self-administer
- Physician approves

**When staff administration is necessary:**
- Cognitive impairment affecting memory or judgment
- Risk of over-dosing or missing doses
- Physician orders staff administration
- Resident requests staff assistance

**Most residents with dementia will need staff administration for safety, but this should be assessed individually, not assumed.**

### Right 12: To Refuse Treatment

**What it means:**
Residents can refuse medical treatment, medications, therapy, or procedures. They have the right to make their own healthcare decisions.

**In practice:**
- Resident can refuse medications
- Can refuse to go to doctor appointments
- Can decline therapy or treatments
- Can refuse personal care (though you should document and address underlying reasons)

**Informed refusal:**
- Ensure resident understands consequences of refusal
- Notify supervisor and physician
- Document refusal and education provided
- Continue to offer care/treatment

**Capacity considerations:**
- Residents with dementia may lack capacity for complex medical decisions
- Legal guardian or power of attorney may make decisions
- Even with guardianship, resident preferences should be honored when possible
- Advance directives (living wills, DNR orders) guide end-of-life care

**What you should do when care is refused:**
1. Respect the refusal immediately (don't force)
2. Try to understand why (pain, fear, timing, approach?)
3. Offer alternatives or try later
4. Document the refusal and your response
5. Notify supervisor if significant (medication refusal, declining food/water)

**Example:**
"Mrs. Williams, it's time for your bath."
"I don't want a bath today."

**Appropriate response:** "That's okay, Mrs. Williams. Would you like to take one this evening instead? Or would you prefer a shower tomorrow morning?" (Offering alternatives while respecting refusal)

**Inappropriate response:** "You have to take a bath. You haven't bathed in two days." (Forcing care)

### Right 13: To Be Informed of Their Rights

**What it means:**
Residents must be told about their rights in language they understand, and staff must ensure they know how to exercise these rights.

**In practice:**
- Provide written rights statement at admission
- Explain rights verbally
- Post rights in common areas
- Review rights regularly
- Ensure information is accessible (large print, language translation, verbal explanation)

**Your role:**
- Know the rights yourself (that's why you're taking this training!)
- Remind residents of their rights when relevant
- Support residents in exercising rights
- Provide information about how to file complaints

**For residents with cognitive impairment:**
- Still entitled to know their rights
- May need repeated reminders
- Family/guardians should be informed
- Rights still apply even if resident can't fully understand them

### Right 14: To File Grievances Without Retaliation

**What it means:**
Residents can complain about care, staff, conditions, or rights violations without fear of punishment or retaliation.

**In practice:**
- Residents can complain to facility management
- Can file complaints with LARA
- Can contact Long-Term Care Ombudsman (866-485-9393)
- Can report to Adult Protective Services
- Cannot be punished, threatened, or treated differently for complaining

**Retaliation is illegal:**
- Can't threaten discharge for complaints
- Can't provide worse care or ignore resident
- Can't ridicule or embarrass resident for complaining
- Can't deny services or privileges

**Your response to complaints:**
- Listen respectfully
- Thank resident for bringing concern to your attention
- Don't get defensive or make excuses
- Address the concern if you can
- Notify supervisor
- Document the complaint and resolution
- Follow up with resident

**Encourage feedback:**
Create a culture where residents feel comfortable expressing concerns. Complaints are opportunities to improve care, not personal attacks.

### Right 15: To Be Informed of Services and Costs

**What it means:**
Residents must be told what services are provided, what they cost, and what changes will occur in services or fees.

**In practice:**
- Written service agreement at admission
- Clear explanation of what's included in base rate
- Notice of additional services and costs
- Advanced notice of fee increases
- Itemized billing available

**No surprise charges:**
- Can't add fees without notice
- Can't charge for services included in base rate
- Can't require purchases from facility vendors
- Must disclose all costs upfront

**Your role:**
- Refer billing questions to administrator
- Don't make commitments about costs
- Don't pressure residents to buy additional services
- Report if you hear about unexpected charges

### Right 16: To Have Access to Their Records

**What it means:**
Residents can review their own medical records, service plans, and other documentation about their care.

**In practice:**
- Resident can request to see their file
- Must be provided access within reasonable time
- Can request copies (may charge reasonable copying fee)
- Can request corrections to inaccurate information

**Your role:**
- If resident asks to see records, notify supervisor (don't just hand over files)
- Supervisor will facilitate access
- Never alter or remove records because resident might see them
- Document accurately knowing resident may read it

**Related to this right:**
- Residents can have their physician of choice
- Can request second opinions
- Can participate in care planning
- Can have advocate or family member review records with them

### Right 17: To Be Free from Discrimination

**What it means:**
Residents cannot be discriminated against based on race, color, religion, national origin, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, age, disability, or other protected characteristics.

**In practice:**
- Equal access to all services and activities
- Equal quality of care regardless of who the resident is
- Respect for diverse identities, cultures, and beliefs
- Accommodation of disabilities
- No segregation or different treatment

**In practice:**
- Provide same quality care to all residents
- Respect religious practices (dietary needs, prayer times, holidays)
- Use correct pronouns and names for LGBTQ+ residents
- Accommodate cultural preferences
- Ensure activities include diverse interests
- Don't make assumptions based on stereotypes

**Common discrimination issues:**
- Providing better care to "favorite" residents
- Ignoring residents who are difficult or have communication barriers
- Mocking accents or cultural practices
- Disrespecting religious beliefs or practices
- Treating LGBTQ+ residents differently

**Remember:** Your personal beliefs don't matter in professional care. You must provide respectful, quality care to ALL residents equally.

## Rights in Daily Practice: Case Studies

### Case Study 1: Balancing Rights and Safety

**Scenario:**
Mr. Peterson has Alzheimer's disease and frequently tries to leave the building, stating he needs to "go to work" (he's been retired for 15 years). The facility has door alarms to alert staff when he tries to leave. His daughter wants staff to "lock him in his room at night" so he doesn't wander.

**Rights considerations:**
- Right to freedom of movement (Right #8)
- Right to be free from restraints (Right #10)
- Right to dignity and respect (Right #1)
- Right to safety and protection from harm

**Appropriate approach:**
- Door alarms are acceptable—they alert staff but don't restrain Mr. Peterson
- Locking him in his room is a restraint and violates his rights
- Use least restrictive interventions:
  - Redirect when he tries to leave ("Mr. Peterson, it's evening now. Work is finished for today. Let's have dinner.")
  - Provide meaningful activities to reduce restlessness
  - Identify triggers for wandering (time of day, boredom, toileting needs)
  - Structured walking programs or secure outdoor space
  - Validation approach ("Tell me about your work")

**Explain to daughter:**
"We understand your concern for your father's safety. Locking him in his room would violate his rights and could increase his agitation. We use door alarms and staff supervision, along with activities and redirection to keep him safe while respecting his dignity and freedom."

### Case Study 2: Right to Refuse Care

**Scenario:**
Mrs. Williams has refused to shower for over a week. She says, "I don't need a shower. Leave me alone." She's starting to have body odor, and another resident complained. Staff are frustrated because "she's being difficult."

**Rights considerations:**
- Right to refuse treatment/care (Right #12)
- Right to dignity (Right #1)
- Other residents' right to pleasant environment

**Inappropriate response:**
- Forcing her into the shower
- Telling her she smells bad and has to bathe
- Giving up and not addressing hygiene

**Appropriate approach:**
1. **Investigate reasons for refusal:**
   - Is the shower frightening? (common in dementia—water can be scary)
   - Is she in pain during transfers or movement?
   - Does she feel cold in the bathroom?
   - Is there a privacy concern?
   - Cultural bathing preferences?

2. **Offer alternatives:**
   - Bed bath instead of shower
   - Different time of day
   - Different staff member (same gender?)
   - Warmer bathroom
   - Play favorite music during bath

3. **Use person-centered approaches:**
   - "Mrs. Williams, I brought some of that lavender soap you like. Would you like to freshen up before lunch?"
   - Hand-under-hand technique (Teepa Snow method)
   - Make bathing pleasant, not a chore

4. **Document:**
   - Refusal and reasons (if known)
   - Alternatives offered
   - Plan for addressing hygiene

**Balance:**
Respect her right to refuse while addressing hygiene creatively. If she continues to refuse all hygiene care and it poses health risk, involve nurse, physician, and family in care planning.

### Case Study 3: Privacy vs. Safety Monitoring

**Scenario:**
Mrs. Chen has diabetes and sometimes becomes confused when her blood sugar drops. Last week she fell when getting up at night. The nurse wants to do bed checks every 2 hours throughout the night. Mrs. Chen says, "I feel like I'm in prison. Can't I have any privacy?"

**Rights considerations:**
- Right to privacy (Right #3)
- Right to safety
- Right to be free from neglect (Right #2)

**Inappropriate approach:**
- Ignoring her concern: "It's for your safety. We have to do it."
- Stopping all monitoring and risking her safety

**Appropriate approach:**
1. **Involve Mrs. Chen in problem-solving:**
   - Explain the safety concern clearly
   - Ask her input: "Mrs. Chen, we're worried about another fall. What would help you feel safe but also respect your privacy?"

2. **Explore alternatives:**
   - Bed alarm that alerts staff if she gets up (less intrusive than constant checks)
   - Motion sensor night light
   - Keep phone/call button within reach
   - Lower bed to reduce fall injury risk
   - Bedside commode to reduce walking at night
   - Better blood sugar management to prevent drops

3. **Negotiate frequency:**
   - Perhaps checks at 11 PM and 3 AM instead of every 2 hours
   - Use quiet knock and quick visual check rather than full room entry
   - Time checks around natural awakening (many elderly wake to use bathroom)

4. **Document in service plan:**
   - The safety concern
   - Mrs. Chen's preferences
   - Agreed-upon monitoring plan
   - Less restrictive alternatives tried
   - Regular review of continued need

**Key principle:** Use the LEAST restrictive intervention necessary to ensure safety. Involve the resident in decision-making.

### Case Study 4: Financial Exploitation Suspicion

**Scenario:**
Mr. Lopez usually buys treats and small gifts for other residents. Recently he says he "doesn't have any money anymore." His nephew has been visiting frequently and asking staff when the mail arrives. You saw the nephew take an envelope from Mr. Lopez's room last week.

**Rights considerations:**
- Right to control own finances (Right #4)
- Right to be free from abuse (financial exploitation) (Right #2)

**What you must do:**
1. **Document your observations objectively:**
   - Date/time of nephew's visits
   - What you observed (nephew taking envelope)
   - Mr. Lopez's statements about not having money
   - Any other concerning behaviors

2. **Report to supervisor immediately**

3. **Report to Adult Protective Services (855-444-3911):**
   - Financial exploitation is abuse
   - You are a mandatory reporter
   - APS will investigate

4. **Do NOT:**
   - Accuse the nephew directly
   - Investigate yourself
   - Tell Mr. Lopez you're reporting (unless he asks)
   - Ignore the situation

**Follow-up:**
- APS may interview resident and family
- Facility may need to provide additional safeguards for resident's money
- Possible legal action if exploitation confirmed

**Remember:** Protecting residents' rights includes protecting them from those who would violate their rights, even if it's family.

## Special Considerations: Rights and Dementia

### Unique Challenges

Residents with dementia face unique rights protection challenges:

**Cognitive impairment doesn't eliminate rights:**
- Person with dementia still has all legal rights
- May need support to exercise rights
- Family/guardian involvement may be necessary
- But don't assume incapacity—assess individually

**Communication barriers:**
- May not be able to express preferences verbally
- Need staff to recognize non-verbal communication
- Behavior IS communication

**Capacity fluctuates:**
- May have "good days" and "bad days"
- May have capacity for some decisions but not others
- Can have preferences even without full decision-making capacity

### How to Protect Rights for Residents with Dementia

**1. Learn their history and preferences:**
- Life story work with family
- Document past preferences, routines, values
- Use this information to honor choices they would have made

**2. Offer choices at appropriate level:**
- May not be able to choose between 10 activities, but can choose between 2
- Can choose blue shirt or red shirt even if can't remember what day it is
- Structure choices to maximize autonomy

**3. Respect refusals:**
- "No" is still meaningful even with dementia
- Pushing away, turning away, crying = refusal
- Try different approach or time rather than forcing

**4. Maintain dignity:**
- Speak respectfully (not baby talk)
- Provide privacy during care
- Protect from embarrassment
- Honor personhood

**5. Recognize retained abilities:**
- Focus on what they CAN do, not just losses
- Support independence in remaining abilities
- Don't take over tasks they can still do (even if slower)

**Example:**
Mrs. Anderson can no longer manage her medications safely (poor judgment, forgets doses). This right (#11 - self-administer medication) must be limited for safety. However, you can still respect her autonomy by:
- Explaining medications when giving them
- Asking if she'd like water or juice with pills
- Letting her hold the cup and take pills herself (you just provide them at the right time)
- Not forcing if she refuses—try again later or offer choice ("Would you like these now or after breakfast?")

## The Role of Advocacy Organizations

### Michigan Long-Term Care Ombudsman

**What is an Ombudsman?**
An independent advocate who investigates complaints, resolves problems, and protects rights of residents in long-term care facilities.

**Contact:** 866-485-9393

**What Ombudsman can do:**
- Investigate complaints about care, rights violations, or facility conditions
- Mediate disputes between residents and facilities
- Provide information about rights
- Advocate for residents
- Monitor facilities for quality of care
- Report serious violations to LARA

**When to contact Ombudsman:**
- Resident has a complaint that facility hasn't resolved
- Suspected rights violations
- Concerns about quality of care
- Discharge disputes
- Financial concerns

**Your role:**
- Inform residents of their right to contact Ombudsman
- Provide Ombudsman contact information
- Cooperate with Ombudsman investigations
- Don't discourage residents from contacting Ombudsman

### Adult Protective Services (APS)

**Contact:** 855-444-3911

**Handles:**
- Reports of abuse, neglect, or exploitation
- Investigations of vulnerable adult mistreatment
- Protective interventions
- Legal action when necessary

**You are a mandatory reporter** - you must report suspected abuse to APS (covered in Module 7).

### Michigan LARA (Licensing and Regulatory Affairs)

**Contact:** 866-856-0126

**Handles:**
- Licensing and regulation of AFC facilities
- Complaint investigations
- Facility inspections
- Enforcement actions
- Citations and penalties

**Residents can file complaints directly with LARA** if they believe the facility is violating regulations or their rights.

## How Residents Can File Grievances

### Internal Grievance Process

Every AFC facility must have a grievance procedure. Typically:

1. **Verbal complaint to staff or supervisor**
2. **Written complaint to administrator**
3. **Facility investigation and response**
4. **Resolution or corrective action**

**Timeline:** Facilities should respond within reasonable time (usually 7-14 days for formal written complaints).

### External Complaints

If internal process doesn't resolve the issue, residents can contact:

**Long-Term Care Ombudsman:** 866-485-9393
**Michigan LARA:** 866-856-0126
**Adult Protective Services (if abuse/neglect):** 855-444-3911

### Retaliation is Prohibited

**Residents cannot be punished for filing complaints.** This includes:
- Threats of discharge
- Reduced quality of care
- Ignoring or isolating the resident
- Ridicule or embarrassment
- Any other negative consequences

**If you witness retaliation, report it immediately.** This is a serious violation.

## Your Responsibility: Rights Protection in Action

### Daily Rights-Based Practice

**Morning routine example:**

❌ **Rights-violating approach:**
"Time to get up, Mrs. Johnson. Let's get you dressed." (Enters room without knocking, opens curtains, begins removing nightgown without asking)

✅ **Rights-respecting approach:**
(Knock on door) "Mrs. Johnson, good morning! May I come in? Did you sleep well? Would you like to get up now or rest a bit longer? What would you like to wear today?" (Closes door for privacy during dressing)

**Rights honored:**
- Privacy (knocking, closing door)
- Dignity and respect (pleasant greeting, asking permission)
- Choice (when to get up, what to wear)

### Recognizing Rights Violations

**Be alert for these warning signs:**

**By staff:**
- Rough handling or impatient care
- Ignoring call lights or requests
- Taking resident belongings
- Restricting visitors without cause
- Forcing care on residents who refuse
- Discussing residents in public areas
- Disrespectful language or tone

**By family/visitors:**
- Taking resident money or belongings
- Isolating resident from others
- Pressuring resident about finances or living arrangements
- Verbal abuse or threats
- Inappropriate control over resident's decisions

**By other residents:**
- Bullying or intimidation
- Taking belongings
- Unwanted touching
- Verbal abuse

**What to do:**
- Intervene if safe (stop the behavior)
- Report to supervisor immediately
- Document objectively
- Report to APS if abuse/neglect (855-444-3911)
- Support the affected resident

## Balancing Rights with Responsibilities

### The Social Contract

Rights come with responsibilities. In a community living setting:

**Residents' responsibilities:**
- Respect other residents' rights
- Follow reasonable facility rules (meal times, quiet hours)
- Treat staff respectfully
- Pay agreed-upon fees
- Not endanger others

**When one person's rights conflict with another's:**
Example: Mr. Davis likes to play music loudly (his right to personal choice and activities). Mrs. Chen has the right to peace and quiet in her room next door.

**Resolution:**
- Negotiate compromise (headphones, designated music times, volume limits)
- Mediate between residents
- Consider room changes if needed
- Document agreed-upon solution

**Facility can set reasonable rules:**
- Quiet hours at night
- No smoking indoors
- Visitor hours (though should be generous)
- Safety rules (no candles, etc.)

**Rules must be:**
- Reasonable and necessary
- Applied equally to all residents
- Respectful of rights
- Clearly communicated
- Not punitive

## Key Takeaways

### Remember These Essential Points:

1. **Michigan law guarantees 17 specific resident rights** (R 400.14304) - know them, respect them, protect them.

2. **Rights are not privileges** - they are legal protections. Residents don't have to "earn" rights through good behavior.

3. **Cognitive impairment doesn't eliminate rights** - residents with dementia retain all rights and may need your advocacy to exercise them.

4. **You are a rights protector** - your daily interactions either honor or violate rights. Choose to honor them.

5. **Right to refuse is fundamental** - residents can refuse care, treatment, activities. Respect refusals and seek to understand underlying reasons.

6. **Privacy and dignity are paramount** - knock before entering, close doors during care, speak respectfully, protect confidentiality.

7. **Freedom from abuse and neglect is absolute** - report suspected violations immediately to supervisor and APS (855-444-3911).

8. **Residents can file grievances without retaliation** - support residents in voicing concerns. Complaints are opportunities to improve.

9. **Balancing rights and safety requires least restrictive approaches** - always use the minimum restriction necessary, involve residents in decisions.

10. **Know advocacy resources:**
    - Long-Term Care Ombudsman: 866-485-9393
    - Adult Protective Services: 855-444-3911
    - Michigan LARA: 866-856-0126
