## Part I: Understanding Infection and Disease Transmission

### Why Infection Control Matters in AFC Homes

Older adults, especially those with dementia or chronic conditions, have weakened immune systems and are at higher risk for infections. Infections can cause serious complications, hospitalizations, and even death in this vulnerable population.¹

**Your role in infection control is critical:**
- Prevent infections from entering the AFC home
- Prevent spread of infections between residents
- Prevent spread from residents to staff and families
- Recognize infections early for prompt treatment
- Protect yourself from occupational exposure

**The impact of good infection control:**
- Fewer resident illnesses and hospitalizations
- Better quality of life for residents
- Lower healthcare costs
- Safer workplace for staff
- Compliance with regulations²

### The Chain of Infection

Understanding how infections spread helps you know where to intervene. The **chain of infection** has six links—break any link, and infection cannot spread.³

**Link 1: Infectious Agent (Pathogen)**
The microorganism that causes disease: bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites

**Link 2: Reservoir**
Where the pathogen lives and multiplies: people, animals, equipment, environment (water, soil, surfaces)

**Link 3: Portal of Exit**
How the pathogen leaves the reservoir: respiratory secretions (coughing, sneezing), blood, urine, feces, wound drainage, saliva

**Link 4: Mode of Transmission**
How the pathogen travels from reservoir to new host:
- **Contact** (direct or indirect) - touching contaminated surfaces or people
- **Droplet** - respiratory droplets from coughing/sneezing (travel 3-6 feet)
- **Airborne** - tiny particles that remain suspended in air and travel long distances
- **Vehicle** - contaminated food, water, medications, or equipment
- **Vector** - insects or animals (ticks, mosquitoes)

**Link 5: Portal of Entry**
How the pathogen enters a new host: mucous membranes (eyes, nose, mouth), broken skin, respiratory tract, urinary tract, bloodstream

**Link 6: Susceptible Host**
A person who can get sick: older adults, people with weakened immunity, chronic diseases, malnutrition, stress

**Breaking the chain:**
- Eliminate pathogens (cleaning, disinfection)
- Control reservoirs (isolation, proper waste disposal)
- Block portals of exit (covering coughs, wound care)
- Interrupt transmission (hand hygiene, PPE, environmental cleaning)
- Protect portals of entry (intact skin, hand hygiene before eating/touching face)
- Strengthen host resistance (nutrition, vaccinations, rest)⁴

## Part II: Hand Hygiene - The Single Most Important Infection Control Measure

### Why Hand Hygiene Works

**Hand hygiene (handwashing or alcohol-based hand sanitizer) is the #1 way to prevent infection spread.** Research shows that proper hand hygiene can reduce infections by 50% or more.⁵

**How it works:**
Your hands pick up pathogens from surfaces, residents, and equipment. When you touch your face, prepare food, or touch another resident without cleaning your hands, you transfer those pathogens.

**Handwashing physically removes:**
- Dirt and organic material
- Transient microorganisms (picked up from environment)
- Some resident microorganisms (normally on skin)

**Alcohol-based hand sanitizer kills:**
- Most bacteria and viruses quickly (if hands aren't visibly soiled)

### When to Perform Hand Hygiene

**The CDC and WHO recommend hand hygiene in these situations (at minimum):**⁶

**Before:**
- Touching a resident
- Performing any care activity
- Preparing, handling, or serving food
- Eating or drinking
- Putting on gloves
- Any clean/aseptic procedure (wound care, medication administration)

**After:**
- Touching a resident
- Contact with blood, body fluids, secretions, excretions, mucous membranes, broken skin, or wound dressings
- Removing gloves or other PPE
- Touching contaminated surfaces (bedpans, urinals, soiled linens)
- Using the restroom
- Blowing your nose, coughing, or sneezing
- Handling garbage or dirty laundry
- Touching your face, hair, or phone

**General rule: When in doubt, clean your hands.**

### Proper Handwashing Technique

**When to use soap and water (required):**⁷
- Hands are visibly dirty or soiled
- After using the restroom
- After contact with *Clostridioides difficile* (C. diff) - alcohol doesn't kill the spores
- Before eating
- After handling food

**Proper technique (total time: 20 seconds minimum):**

1. **Wet** hands with clean running water (warm or cold)
2. **Apply** soap and lather well
3. **Scrub** all surfaces:
   - Palms and backs of hands
   - Between all fingers
   - Under fingernails
   - Wrists
4. **Scrub for at least 20 seconds** (hum "Happy Birthday" twice)
5. **Rinse** thoroughly under running water
6. **Dry** with clean paper towel or air dryer
7. **Turn off faucet** with paper towel (don't re-contaminate clean hands)

**Common mistakes:**
- Rushing (less than 20 seconds)
- Missing areas (thumbs, fingertips, wrists)
- Not using enough soap
- Not drying hands completely (wet hands spread more germs)

### Alcohol-Based Hand Sanitizer

**When to use hand sanitizer:**
- When hands are not visibly soiled
- Between resident care activities if no visible contamination
- When soap and water aren't immediately available
- Multiple times during care if hands aren't soiled

**Proper technique:**
1. Apply product to palm of one hand (amount per manufacturer directions, usually dime to quarter size)
2. Rub hands together, covering all surfaces (same areas as handwashing)
3. Rub until hands are completely dry (usually 20-30 seconds)
4. Don't rinse or wipe off—let it dry naturally

**Requirements for effectiveness:**⁸
- Must contain at least 60% alcohol
- Hands must not be visibly soiled
- Must use enough product
- Must rub until completely dry

### Hand Hygiene Barriers and Solutions

**Common barriers:**
- "I'm too busy"
- Skin irritation from frequent washing
- Sinks not conveniently located
- Forgetting between tasks

**Solutions:**
- Make it a habit—automatic, not optional
- Use lotion regularly to prevent dry skin
- Place hand sanitizer dispensers throughout facility
- Post reminders at key locations
- Model good practice for others

## Part III: Standard Precautions

### What Are Standard Precautions?

**Standard precautions** are infection control practices used with ALL residents, ALL the time, regardless of known infection status. They're based on the principle that any resident could have an infection (known or unknown), so you always protect yourself and others.⁹

**Standard precautions were developed by the CDC** and are required in all healthcare settings, including Adult Foster Care homes.

### Components of Standard Precautions

**1. Hand Hygiene**
(Covered above—the foundation of all infection control)

**2. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)**
Use appropriate barriers based on anticipated exposure:
- **Gloves** - when touching blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, broken skin, or contaminated items
- **Gown/apron** - when clothing may become contaminated during care
- **Mask** - when splashes/sprays of body fluids are possible or when resident has respiratory infection
- **Eye protection** - when splashes to eyes are possible
- **Face shield** - combines mask and eye protection

**3. Respiratory Hygiene/Cough Etiquette**
- Cover coughs and sneezes with tissue (dispose immediately)
- If no tissue, cough into elbow/sleeve (not hands)
- Perform hand hygiene after coughing/sneezing
- Offer masks to residents with respiratory symptoms
- Maintain distance (3-6 feet) when possible

**4. Safe Injection Practices**
(Less common in AFC, but if medications are given by injection):
- Use sterile, single-use needles and syringes
- Never reuse or share between residents
- Proper sharps disposal

**5. Safe Handling of Equipment and Environment**
- Clean and disinfect shared equipment between residents
- Clean and disinfect environmental surfaces regularly
- Handle soiled linens carefully to prevent contamination

**6. Proper Handling of Laundry**
- Handle soiled linens with minimal agitation
- Don't sort or rinse in resident care areas
- Wear gloves when handling soiled laundry
- Wash in hot water with detergent

**7. Waste Disposal**
- Use appropriate containers for regular trash vs. biohazard waste
- Don't overfill waste containers
- Dispose of sharps in puncture-resistant containers¹⁰

## Part IV: Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

### Selecting Appropriate PPE

The type of PPE you use depends on the **anticipated exposure** during the care activity.¹¹

**Ask yourself:**
- Will I touch blood or body fluids?
- Could body fluids splash or spray?
- Will my clothing get contaminated?
- Does the resident have a contagious infection?

**PPE selection guide:**

| Care Activity | Gloves | Gown | Mask | Eye Protection |
|--------------|--------|------|------|----------------|
| Taking vital signs (no contact with body fluids) | No | No | No | No |
| Assisting with eating | No* | No | No | No |
| Bathing/Showering | Yes | Yes** | No | No |
| Toileting/Changing briefs | Yes | Yes** | No | No |
| Wound care | Yes | Yes** | Mask*** | Eye protection*** |
| Caring for resident with respiratory infection | Yes | No | Yes | No |
| Cleaning up vomit, blood, or body fluid spills | Yes | Yes | Mask | Eye protection |

*Use gloves if you have cuts/open areas on hands
**Gown/apron if clothing may get wet or soiled
***If splashing is possible

### Proper Use of Gloves

**When to wear gloves:**
- Anticipating contact with blood, body fluids, secretions, excretions, mucous membranes, or broken skin
- Touching contaminated items or surfaces
- Performing care that requires them per facility policy

**Proper glove use:**¹²
1. **Perform hand hygiene before putting on gloves**
2. **Select appropriate size** (too small = tears; too large = poor dexterity)
3. **Put on gloves just before care activity**
4. **Change gloves between residents** (never reuse)
5. **Change gloves during care if they become torn or heavily contaminated**
6. **Remove and dispose of gloves after care**
7. **Perform hand hygiene immediately after removing gloves**

**Common mistakes:**
- Wearing gloves for extended periods
- Touching clean surfaces while wearing contaminated gloves
- Not changing gloves between residents
- Not washing hands after removing gloves (gloves can have microscopic holes)
- Wearing gloves in hallways or common areas

**Important: Gloves do NOT replace hand hygiene. Always wash hands before and after glove use.**

### Putting On (Donning) and Removing (Doffing) PPE

**Order for putting ON PPE (Donning):**¹³
1. **Gown** - Tie in back, ensure full coverage
2. **Mask** - Secure ties or ear loops, mold nosepiece
3. **Eye protection** - Position over eyes or face
4. **Gloves** - Extend over gown cuffs

**Order for taking OFF PPE (Doffing - most contaminated first):**
1. **Gloves** - Peel off, turning inside out; dispose
2. **Eye protection** - Handle by ear pieces or headband only; place in designated area for cleaning
3. **Gown** - Unfasten, pull away from body, roll inside out; dispose
4. **Mask** - Remove by touching only ties/ear loops (don't touch front); dispose
5. **HAND HYGIENE** - immediately after removing all PPE

**Key principle:** Remove PPE carefully to avoid contaminating yourself, then ALWAYS perform hand hygiene.

## Part V: Common Infections in Older Adults

### Respiratory Infections

**Types:**
- Influenza (flu)
- Pneumonia (bacterial or viral)
- COVID-19
- Common cold
- Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)

**Why older adults are at risk:**
- Weakened immune systems
- Chronic lung conditions (COPD, asthma)
- Decreased cough reflex
- Immobility (fluid can pool in lungs)
- Difficulty clearing secretions¹⁴

**Signs and symptoms:**
- Cough (with or without sputum)
- Fever or chills (note: older adults may not spike high fevers)
- Shortness of breath or rapid breathing
- Fatigue or weakness
- Confusion (may be the only symptom in older adults)
- Loss of appetite

**Prevention:**
- Annual flu vaccine
- Pneumonia vaccine (pneumococcal)
- COVID-19 vaccination and boosters
- Respiratory hygiene (cover coughs, masks when ill)
- Hand hygiene
- Isolation of sick residents when possible
- Good ventilation
- Encourage deep breathing exercises and mobility

**Your response:**
- Report symptoms to nurse/supervisor immediately
- Isolate resident if possible (or cohort with others who have same illness)
- Use droplet precautions (mask, maintain distance)
- Monitor for worsening symptoms
- Encourage fluids and rest

### Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs)

**Why older adults are at risk:**
- Incomplete bladder emptying
- Use of urinary catheters
- Incontinence and moisture
- Dehydration
- Weakened immune system
- Anatomical changes with aging
- In women: proximity of urethra to rectum¹⁵

**Signs and symptoms:**
- Burning or pain with urination
- Frequent urination or urgency
- Cloudy, dark, or strong-smelling urine
- Blood in urine
- Lower abdominal or pelvic pain
- **In older adults with dementia: Sudden confusion, agitation, or behavior change may be the only sign**
- Fever (may be absent in older adults)

**Prevention:**
- Adequate hydration (offer fluids regularly)
- Toileting on schedule (don't leave in soiled briefs)
- Front-to-back wiping for women
- Good perineal hygiene
- Avoid catheters when possible; if used, proper catheter care
- Cranberry products (some evidence of benefit)

**Your response:**
- Report symptoms immediately
- Encourage increased fluids
- Assist with frequent toileting
- Monitor output
- Collect urine sample if ordered

### Skin Infections

**Types:**
- Cellulitis (bacterial skin infection)
- Pressure ulcers that become infected
- Fungal infections (athlete's foot, yeast infections in skin folds)
- Scabies (contagious skin mite infestation)
- Methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA)

**Why older adults are at risk:**
- Thin, fragile skin that tears easily
- Poor circulation
- Immobility (pressure on skin)
- Moisture from incontinence
- Diabetes (slows healing)
- Malnutrition¹⁶

**Signs and symptoms:**
- Redness, warmth, swelling
- Pain or tenderness
- Open sores or wounds
- Drainage or pus
- Foul odor
- Fever
- Rash or itching (fungal or scabies)

**Prevention:**
- Keep skin clean and dry
- Change wet/soiled clothing and briefs promptly
- Use moisturizer to prevent dry, cracked skin
- Inspect skin daily during bathing/dressing
- Reposition immobile residents every 2 hours
- Proper nutrition and hydration
- Treat minor skin breaks immediately

**Your response:**
- Report any skin changes immediately
- Use contact precautions for draining wounds or MRSA
- Follow wound care orders precisely
- Keep infected areas covered

### Gastrointestinal Infections

**Types:**
- *Clostridioides difficile* (C. diff) - causes severe diarrhea
- Norovirus - highly contagious "stomach bug"
- Food poisoning (various bacteria)

**Why older adults are at risk:**
- Antibiotic use (kills good bacteria, allows C. diff to grow)
- Weakened immune system
- Living in group settings (easy spread)
- Medications that slow GI motility¹⁷

**Signs and symptoms:**
- Diarrhea (watery, frequent)
- Nausea and vomiting
- Abdominal cramping or pain
- Fever
- Loss of appetite
- Dehydration (dry mouth, decreased urine, weakness, confusion)

**Prevention:**
- Hand hygiene (SOAP AND WATER for C. diff - alcohol doesn't kill spores)
- Proper food storage and handling
- Clean and disinfect bathrooms thoroughly
- Isolation of ill residents
- Use of bleach-based disinfectants for C. diff

**Your response:**
- Report symptoms immediately (diarrhea can cause rapid dehydration)
- Isolate resident
- Contact precautions (gloves and gown for all care)
- HANDWASHING with soap and water (not sanitizer) for C. diff
- Monitor for dehydration
- Clean and disinfect bathroom and surfaces with bleach solution

### COVID-19 and Emerging Infectious Diseases

**Lessons from the COVID-19 pandemic:**
- Older adults and those with chronic conditions are at highest risk for severe illness
- Respiratory infections spread quickly in group living settings
- Infection control basics (hand hygiene, masks, distancing) save lives
- Vaccination is critical protection
- Early detection and isolation prevent outbreaks¹⁸

**Ongoing COVID-19 precautions:**
- Stay updated on current CDC and Michigan LARA guidelines
- Ensure residents receive COVID-19 vaccinations and boosters
- Test symptomatic residents promptly
- Isolate positive cases
- Use appropriate PPE
- Monitor for new variants and adjust precautions as needed

**Preparing for future outbreaks:**
- Maintain supplies of PPE, disinfectants, and hand hygiene products
- Have outbreak response plans in place
- Stay informed about emerging infectious diseases
- Build infection control into daily routines, not just during outbreaks

## Part VI: Environmental Cleaning and Disinfection

### Cleaning vs. Disinfecting

**Cleaning** = Physically removing dirt, debris, and some microorganisms using soap/detergent and water. This reduces the number of germs but doesn't kill all of them.¹⁹

**Disinfecting** = Using chemicals to kill microorganisms on surfaces. This kills most germs but requires prior cleaning for effectiveness (disinfectants can't penetrate dirt and organic material).

**Best practice: Clean FIRST, then disinfect.**

### High-Touch Surfaces

**High-touch surfaces** should be cleaned and disinfected multiple times per day (at least twice, more often if visibly soiled or during outbreaks).²⁰

**Examples of high-touch surfaces:**
- Doorknobs and handles
- Light switches
- Handrails
- Bathroom faucets, toilet handles, grab bars
- TV remotes
- Phones
- Tabletops
- Bed rails
- Wheelchair armrests and controls
- Kitchen counters and appliances

### Proper Cleaning and Disinfecting Procedure

**Steps:**²¹
1. **Put on gloves** (and other PPE if needed)
2. **Clean surface** with detergent/soap and water to remove visible dirt
3. **Rinse and dry** if needed
4. **Apply EPA-registered disinfectant** according to product label
5. **Allow proper contact time** (usually 1-10 minutes depending on product—read label!)
6. **Don't wipe off immediately** - wet contact time is required to kill germs
7. **Allow to air dry** or wipe after contact time
8. **Dispose of cleaning materials** properly
9. **Remove gloves and perform hand hygiene**

**Common mistakes:**
- Not reading product labels (contact time, dilution)
- Wiping disinfectant off immediately (doesn't work if not left on surface)
- Using expired products
- Not wearing gloves
- Re-using contaminated cleaning cloths on multiple surfaces

### Disinfectants Commonly Used in AFC Homes

**EPA-registered disinfectants include:**
- Bleach solutions (sodium hypochlorite) - effective against most pathogens including C. diff
- Quaternary ammonium compounds ("quats") - common in commercial products
- Hydrogen peroxide-based products
- Alcohol-based products (for small surfaces)

**Bleach solution preparation:**²²
- For general disinfection: 1000-5000 ppm (usually 1/3 cup bleach per gallon of water)
- For C. diff or outbreaks: 5000 ppm (1:10 dilution - 1.5 cups bleach per gallon of water)
- Mix fresh daily (loses effectiveness after 24 hours)
- Label clearly
- Never mix bleach with other chemicals (creates toxic fumes)

### Laundry Handling

**Proper procedure:**
- Wear gloves when handling soiled laundry
- Handle with minimal agitation (don't shake—this disperses pathogens)
- Don't sort or rinse in resident care areas
- Transport in leak-proof bags
- Wash in hot water (160°F) with detergent, or warm water with bleach
- Dry completely
- Fold clean laundry only after performing hand hygiene
- Store clean linens in clean area, protected from contamination

## Part VII: Bloodborne Pathogens

### What Are Bloodborne Pathogens?

**Bloodborne pathogens** are infectious microorganisms in blood that can cause disease in humans. The most concerning are:²³
- **Hepatitis B virus (HBV)**
- **Hepatitis C virus (HCV)**
- **Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)**

**Routes of transmission:**
- Needlestick or sharps injuries
- Contact with mucous membranes (eyes, nose, mouth)
- Contact with broken skin (cuts, abrasions, dermatitis)
- Blood splashes

### OSHA Bloodborne Pathogens Standard

**OSHA requires employers to:**²⁴
- Develop exposure control plan
- Provide Hepatitis B vaccine at no cost to employees with occupational exposure
- Provide appropriate PPE
- Train employees annually
- Maintain sharps disposal containers
- Have post-exposure protocols

**Your protection:**
- Use standard precautions with all residents
- Treat all blood and body fluids as potentially infectious
- Use PPE appropriately
- Never recap needles
- Dispose of sharps immediately in puncture-resistant containers
- Report exposures immediately

### Exposure Incident Response

**If you have an exposure (needlestick, blood splash to eyes/mouth, etc.):**²⁵

**Immediate action:**
1. **Stop what you're doing**
2. **Wash** affected area immediately:
   - Skin: Wash with soap and water for 15 minutes
   - Eyes: Flush with water or saline for 15 minutes
   - Mouth: Spit out, rinse with water repeatedly
3. **Report immediately** to supervisor
4. **Seek medical evaluation** - post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) must start within hours for effectiveness
5. **Document** the incident (date, time, circumstances, source person if known)

**Follow-up:**
- Medical evaluation of source person (if known and they consent)
- Baseline testing for you
- Post-exposure prophylaxis if indicated
- Follow-up testing at intervals
- Counseling and support

**Prevention is always better than treatment—use standard precautions consistently.**

## Part VIII: Special Considerations for Dementia Care

### Infection Control Challenges with Dementia

Residents with dementia present unique infection control challenges:²⁶
- May not understand or remember infection control instructions
- May touch their face frequently
- May resist handwashing or PPE
- Cannot reliably report symptoms
- May not cover coughs or follow respiratory hygiene
- May touch or handle contaminated items
- May remove PPE (masks, gloves) if placed on them

### Strategies for Success

**Hand hygiene:**
- Make it part of routine (after bathroom, before meals—every time)
- Use gentle guidance and modeling
- Make it pleasant (warm water, nice-smelling soap)
- Keep it simple: "Let's wash our hands before we eat"
- Use hand sanitizer for quick cleaning between activities

**Respiratory hygiene:**
- Keep tissues accessible
- Model covering coughs with elbow
- Gentle reminders: "Cover your mouth"
- May need to isolate if resident can't comply

**Environmental control:**
- More frequent cleaning of high-touch surfaces
- Remove items from rooms during outbreaks
- Simplify environment to reduce contaminated surfaces
- Supervise during activities to prevent hand-to-face contact

**Monitoring for infection:**
- Watch for behavior changes (may be only sign of infection)
- Monitor vital signs regularly
- Don't assume symptoms are "just dementia"
- Report any changes promptly

## Case Studies: Infection Control in Action

### Case Study 1: Preventing a Norovirus Outbreak

**Scenario:**
Monday morning, you arrive at work and find that Mrs. Thompson had vomiting and diarrhea overnight. By midday, two more residents have similar symptoms.

**Initial response (CORRECT):**
1. **Report immediately** to nurse/supervisor
2. **Isolate** affected residents (separate rooms if possible, or cohort together)
3. **Contact precautions** - gloves and gown for all care
4. **Hand hygiene** - soap and water (not just sanitizer)
5. **Environmental cleaning** - disinfect all surfaces in affected rooms and common areas with appropriate disinfectant
6. **Monitor** all other residents for symptoms
7. **Restrict** activities (no group dining or activities until outbreak resolves)
8. **Notify families** of outbreak and visitor restrictions

**What happened:**
By implementing immediate isolation and rigorous infection control, only 3 of 6 residents became ill (vs. potentially all 6). Outbreak resolved in 5 days. No staff became ill.

**Key lesson:** Early detection, immediate isolation, and strict adherence to infection control prevent spread.

---

### Case Study 2: UTI Misidentified as "Just Dementia"

**Scenario:**
Mr. Davis, who has moderate dementia, has been increasingly confused and agitated over the past two days. Staff assume it's "sundowning" or dementia progression. On day 3, he develops a fever and is hospitalized with a severe UTI and sepsis.

**What went wrong:**
- Staff attributed behavior change to dementia, not infection
- No one assessed for other symptoms (urine appearance, discomfort)
- Delayed recognition led to severe illness

**What should have happened:**
- **Any sudden behavior change in person with dementia warrants medical assessment**
- Check for infection signs: urine appearance, temperature, discomfort with urination
- Report changes promptly to nurse
- Early detection and treatment prevent complications

**Key lesson:** In people with dementia, infection often presents as behavior change or increased confusion. Always assess for medical causes when behavior changes suddenly.

---

### Case Study 3: Bloodborne Pathogen Exposure

**Scenario:**
While assisting Mr. Lee with transferring, you notice blood on your hands. Looking down, you see you have a small cut on your finger that you didn't realize was there. Mr. Lee has a skin tear on his arm that's bleeding.

**Immediate response (CORRECT):**
1. **Stop care activity**
2. **Wash hands thoroughly** with soap and water for 15 minutes
3. **Report to supervisor immediately**
4. **Document exposure** (your broken skin contacted resident's blood)
5. **Seek medical evaluation** - risk is lower than needlestick but still requires assessment
6. **Follow employer's post-exposure protocol**

**Prevention for future:**
- **Inspect hands before care** - cover any cuts with waterproof bandage
- **Wear gloves** when you have broken skin, even for low-risk activities
- **Report hand injuries** to supervisor so accommodations can be made

**Key lesson:** Broken skin is a portal of entry for bloodborne pathogens. Always protect open wounds and report exposures immediately.

## Key Takeaways

### Remember These Essential Points:

1. **Hand hygiene is the #1 infection control measure** - clean your hands before and after every resident contact and after any potential contamination.

2. **Standard precautions apply to ALL residents, ALL the time** - treat all blood and body fluids as potentially infectious.

3. **The chain of infection** has six links - breaking any link prevents infection spread.

4. **PPE selection depends on anticipated exposure** - gloves for body fluid contact, gown if clothing will get contaminated, mask for respiratory infections or splashes, eye protection for splashes.

5. **Don and doff PPE in correct order** - don gown first, gloves last; doff gloves first (most contaminated), mask last; always perform hand hygiene after removing PPE.

6. **Common infections in older adults:** Respiratory (flu, pneumonia, COVID-19), UTIs, skin infections, GI infections (C. diff, norovirus).

7. **Behavior changes in dementia may indicate infection** - sudden confusion or agitation warrants medical assessment.

8. **Clean first, then disinfect** - disinfectants require clean surfaces and proper contact time to work.

9. **Bloodborne pathogen exposures require immediate action** - wash/flush for 15 minutes, report immediately, seek medical evaluation.

10. **Infection control protects everyone** - residents, staff, families, and the community.
