## What Are Community Settings?

### Defining Community-Based Care

**Community-based residential care** refers to care provided in home-like settings where people live as part of the community rather than in large institutions. In Michigan, Adult Foster Care (AFC) homes are licensed residential settings that provide supervision, personal care, and protection to adults who need assistance but don't require 24-hour nursing care.

**Key characteristics of community settings:**
- Small, home-like environments (typically 6 residents or fewer in AFC Group Homes)
- Located in regular residential neighborhoods
- Residents maintain connections to community life
- Focus on independence and personal choice
- Individualized care based on each person's needs and preferences

### Why Community Settings Matter

From the 1950s through the 1980s, people with disabilities, mental illness, or chronic conditions were often placed in large state institutions. These facilities housed hundreds or even thousands of people in hospital-like settings, far from their home communities.

Research and advocacy revealed serious problems with institutional care:
- Loss of personal identity and dignity
- Separation from family and community
- Limited personal choice and independence
- Isolation and loneliness
- One-size-fits-all routines

The **deinstitutionalization movement** led to the development of community-based alternatives like Adult Foster Care homes. Today, Michigan's AFC system serves thousands of adults in home-like settings where they can:
- Live in regular neighborhoods
- Maintain family connections
- Make daily choices
- Participate in community activities
- Receive individualized support

## Michigan's Adult Foster Care System

### What is Adult Foster Care?

According to Michigan law (Adult Foster Care Facility Licensing Act, Public Act 218 of 1979), an **Adult Foster Care facility** provides room and board, supervision, and personal care to adults who are not related to the licensee.

**Michigan AFC homes provide:**
- A safe, home-like living environment
- Assistance with activities of daily living (ADLs)
- Supervision and protection
- Nutritious meals
- Medication administration support
- Social activities and engagement
- Connection to healthcare services

### Types of AFC Facilities in Michigan

Michigan licenses several types of AFC facilities:

1. **AFC Family Homes** - 1-6 adults in a family home setting
2. **AFC Group Homes** - 7-12 adults (your setting at Golden Pines)
3. **AFC Small Group Homes** - 13-20 adults
4. **AFC Large Group Homes** - 21+ adults
5. **AFC Congregate Facilities** - Larger facilities with specific requirements

At Golden Pines, you'll work in an **AFC Group Home** licensed for 6 residents, specializing in care for individuals with Alzheimer's disease and other forms of dementia.

### Who Lives in AFC Homes?

AFC homes serve diverse populations with varying needs:

- **Older adults** needing assistance with daily activities
- **Individuals with Alzheimer's disease or dementia**
- People with developmental disabilities
- Adults with mental illness
- Individuals recovering from illness or injury
- People with physical disabilities

**In your role at Golden Pines**, you'll specifically work with residents who have memory loss, confusion, and behavioral changes related to Alzheimer's disease and other dementias. This requires specialized knowledge, patience, and person-centered approaches (which you'll learn throughout this training).

## The Philosophy of Community-Based Care

### Core Principles

**1. Person-Centered Care**
- Each resident is a unique individual with their own history, preferences, values, and needs
- Care plans are tailored to the person, not the person to the care plan
- Residents are active partners in their care decisions
- Focus on abilities, not just limitations

**2. Dignity and Respect**
- All residents deserve to be treated with dignity regardless of their abilities or conditions
- Respect for privacy, personal space, and belongings
- Communication that honors the person's worth and personhood
- Recognition that behavior is communication, especially for those with dementia

**3. Independence and Choice**
- Support residents to do as much for themselves as possible
- Offer meaningful choices throughout the day
- Encourage decision-making even when choices are small
- Balance safety with autonomy

**4. Community Integration**
- AFC homes are part of the neighborhood
- Residents maintain connections to the broader community
- Family and friends are welcomed and encouraged to visit
- Participation in community activities when possible

**5. Quality of Life Focus**
- Physical comfort and health are important, but so are:
  - Emotional well-being
  - Social connections and relationships
  - Meaningful activities and purpose
  - Personal growth and enjoyment

### How Community Settings Differ from Institutions

| **Institutional Care** | **Community-Based Care (AFC)** |
|------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Large facilities (100+ residents) | Small, home-like (6 residents) |
| Hospital-like environment | Residential neighborhood setting |
| Rigid schedules and routines | Flexible, individualized schedules |
| Limited personal choice | Daily choices encouraged |
| Staff-centered care | Person-centered care |
| Isolated from community | Integrated into community |
| Focus on tasks and efficiency | Focus on relationships and quality of life |
| Impersonal "batch care" | Individualized, personalized care |

## Your Role as a Direct Care Worker

### What Does a Direct Care Worker Do?

As a **Direct Care Worker (DCW)** in an AFC home, you are the heart of quality care. You work directly with residents to:

**Daily Care Support:**
- Assist with personal care (bathing, dressing, grooming, toileting)
- Support mobility and transfers
- Help with meals and eating
- Provide medication reminders (after proper training)
- Monitor health status and report changes

**Social and Emotional Support:**
- Build trusting relationships with residents
- Provide companionship and emotional support
- Facilitate social activities and engagement
- Communicate effectively with residents who have dementia
- Respect personal preferences and life history

**Safety and Protection:**
- Maintain a safe environment
- Recognize and report signs of illness or decline
- Follow infection control procedures
- Know emergency procedures
- Identify and report abuse, neglect, or exploitation

**Advocacy and Rights Protection:**
- Understand and protect resident rights
- Support resident choices and preferences
- Communicate resident needs to supervisors and healthcare providers
- Recognize residents as individuals with dignity

### The DCW as a Professional

Working in an AFC home is not "just a job" - it's a professional caregiving role that requires:

- **Knowledge and skills** - This training provides essential competencies
- **Compassion and patience** - Caring for vulnerable adults requires genuine empathy
- **Professionalism** - Maintaining boundaries, confidentiality, and ethical standards
- **Teamwork** - Collaborating with other staff, nurses, families, and healthcare providers
- **Commitment to learning** - Continuing to grow and improve your practice

### Challenges You May Face

Direct care work is rewarding but challenging. Common challenges include:

- **Emotional demands** - Caring for people with progressive diseases like dementia can be emotionally difficult
- **Physical demands** - Assisting with transfers, bathing, and mobility requires physical stamina
- **Behavioral challenges** - Residents with dementia may exhibit aggression, wandering, or resistance to care
- **Communication barriers** - Memory loss and confusion make communication complex
- **Time pressures** - Balancing multiple residents' needs simultaneously

**Good news:** This training will equip you with strategies, techniques, and knowledge to handle these challenges effectively and find fulfillment in your work.

## Case Studies: Understanding Community-Based Care

### Case Study 1: Margaret's Transition

**Background:**
Margaret, 78, lived independently until a series of small strokes affected her memory and balance. Her daughter Sarah worried about Margaret living alone but wanted to avoid a nursing home. Sarah found Golden Pines AFC home.

**What happened:**
- Margaret moved into a private room in a residential neighborhood she recognized
- Staff learned about her love of gardening and bird watching
- Margaret chose when to wake up and what to wear each day
- She helped fold towels and set the table, maintaining a sense of purpose
- Family visited regularly and joined for Sunday dinners

**Outcome:**
Margaret maintained her dignity and independence while receiving needed support. She formed friendships with other residents and staff. Her quality of life improved significantly compared to living alone and isolated.

**Reflection questions:**
- How did the AFC home setting support Margaret's independence?
- What aspects of person-centered care are evident in this case?
- How is this different from institutional care?

### Case Study 2: Robert's Journey

**Background:**
Robert, 72, has early-stage Alzheimer's disease. He worked as a mechanic for 40 years and was very independent. His wife died two years ago, and his memory loss made it unsafe for him to live alone.

**Challenges:**
- Robert initially resisted moving to an AFC home, feeling he didn't need help
- He became frustrated when staff tried to "do everything" for him
- He wandered at night looking for his workshop

**Person-Centered Solutions:**
- Staff learned Robert's life history and his identity as a mechanic
- They created a small workbench area where Robert could tinker with tools (safely)
- Staff encouraged Robert to help with household maintenance tasks
- They used validation techniques when Robert mentioned his wife
- Night staff engaged Robert with photo albums instead of redirecting him to bed

**Outcome:**
Robert's agitation decreased as staff honored his identity and abilities. He found purpose in "fixing things" and helping staff. His sense of dignity was preserved.

**Reflection questions:**
- How did understanding Robert's life history improve his care?
- What would have happened if staff focused only on his limitations?
- How does this demonstrate the difference between task-centered and person-centered care?

## The Legal and Regulatory Framework

### Michigan Adult Foster Care Licensing

The Michigan Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs (LARA) - Bureau of Community and Health Systems oversees AFC homes. Regulations ensure:

- Safe physical environments
- Qualified, trained staff
- Protection of resident rights
- Adequate supervision and care
- Proper medication management
- Nutritious food and meal service

**Key regulation you'll encounter:**
**Michigan Admin Code R 400.14204** - Staff training requirements (that's why you're taking this training!)

### Your Training Requirements

Michigan law requires direct care staff to complete training in **seven core competency areas**:

1. Roles and responsibilities of direct care staff
2. Resident rights and responsibilities
3. Reporting and documentation requirements
4. Preventing and reporting abuse, neglect, and exploitation
5. Basic resident care skills
6. Maintaining a safe environment
7. Responding to resident behaviors

This 16-module training covers all required competencies and more, with specialized focus on dementia care.

## Person-Centered Care: A Deeper Look

### What is Person-Centered Care?

**Person-centered care** means seeing the person first, not the diagnosis or disability. British psychologist **Tom Kitwood** pioneered this approach specifically for dementia care, emphasizing that people with dementia remain full persons deserving of respect, relationships, and quality of life.

**Kitwood's Five Psychological Needs (around Love):**

1. **Comfort** - Warmth, safety, soothing presence
2. **Attachment** - Bonds with others, belonging
3. **Inclusion** - Being part of the community, not isolated
4. **Occupation** - Meaningful activities and purpose
5. **Identity** - Maintaining sense of self, personhood honored

When these needs are met, people with dementia experience greater well-being, reduced agitation, and preserved dignity.

### Person-Centered vs. Task-Centered Care

**Task-centered approach (OLD model):**
- "I need to get Mrs. Johnson bathed by 9am"
- Focus: Completing tasks efficiently
- Resident is passive recipient
- Staff convenience drives decisions
- One-size-fits-all care

**Person-centered approach (CURRENT standard):**
- "Mrs. Johnson prefers to bathe in the evening. How can I make this relaxing for her?"
- Focus: Meeting individual needs and preferences
- Resident is active partner
- Resident preferences drive decisions
- Individualized, flexible care

### Practical Applications in Your Daily Work

**Example 1: Morning Wake-Up**

Task-centered: "Time to get up! We need to get you dressed before breakfast."

Person-centered: *Knock gently* "Good morning, Mr. Williams. Did you sleep well? Would you like to get up now, or rest a bit longer? Coffee is ready when you are."

**Example 2: Bathing**

Task-centered: "It's Tuesday, so it's your shower day. Let's go."

Person-centered: "Mrs. Chen, I know you enjoyed your warm bath yesterday evening. Would you like another bath tonight, or would you prefer a shower this time?"

**Example 3: Activities**

Task-centered: "Everyone to the living room for bingo at 2pm."

Person-centered: "Mr. Davis, I remember you mentioned loving baseball. The Tigers game is on TV this afternoon - would you like to watch? Or would you prefer to sit in the garden?"

## Key Takeaways

### Remember These Essential Points:

1. **Community-based care** provides small, home-like residential settings where residents live as part of the community with individualized support.

2. **Michigan AFC homes** are licensed facilities that provide room, board, supervision, and personal care to adults in home-like environments.

3. **Person-centered care** focuses on the individual's preferences, history, dignity, and quality of life - not just completing tasks.

4. **Your role as a DCW** is professional and essential - you provide direct care, emotional support, safety, and advocacy for residents.

5. **Tom Kitwood's five psychological needs** (Comfort, Attachment, Inclusion, Occupation, Identity) guide quality dementia care.

6. **The shift from institutions to community settings** represents recognition that people deserve dignity, choice, independence, and connection regardless of their care needs.

7. **Michigan regulations** ensure AFC homes maintain safety, protect rights, and provide qualified staff - that's why training like this is required.
