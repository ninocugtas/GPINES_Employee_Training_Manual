## Understanding Activities of Daily Living (ADLs)

### What Are ADLs?

**Activities of Daily Living (ADLs)** are the basic self-care tasks that people perform every day to maintain personal hygiene, health, and well-being.

**The Six Basic ADLs:**
1. **Bathing** - Washing the body
2. **Dressing** - Putting on and taking off clothing
3. **Toileting** - Using the bathroom, managing incontinence
4. **Transferring** - Moving from bed to chair, standing, sitting
5. **Eating** - Getting food from plate to mouth
6. **Continence** - Bladder and bowel control

**Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADLs)** are more complex tasks:
- Meal preparation
- Housekeeping
- Medication management
- Money management
- Transportation
- Phone use

**In Adult Foster Care homes, you'll primarily assist with the six basic ADLs.** Your goal is to support residents in doing as much as possible for themselves while providing assistance where needed.

### Why ADLs Matter

**ADLs measure independence and quality of life.** The more ADLs someone can perform independently, the greater their sense of autonomy, dignity, and self-worth.

**Your role is NOT to do everything for residents, but to:**
- Support them in maintaining their abilities
- Provide assistance only where needed
- Encourage independence and choice
- Adapt tasks to residents' abilities
- Preserve dignity during intimate care

**The tragedy of unnecessary dependence:** When staff do tasks that residents can do themselves (even if slower or messier), residents lose skills through disuse. This is called "learned helplessness" or "excess disability."

**Example:**
Mrs. Johnson can button her shirt but it takes 5 minutes. If staff always button it for her "to save time," within weeks she'll lose the motor skills and confidence to do it herself. Now she's dependent unnecessarily.

## Person-Centered Approaches to Personal Care

### Core Principles

**Before we discuss specific techniques, understand these principles that should guide ALL personal care:**

**1. Preserve dignity during intimate care**
- Close doors and curtains
- Cover body parts not being washed
- Use towels or blankets for modesty
- Don't discuss personal care in public areas
- Treat residents as you'd want to be treated

**2. Promote independence - "Enable, don't disable"**
- Let residents do what they can, even if slower
- Provide tools that promote independence (reachers, sock aids, button hooks)
- Use hand-under-hand technique (Teepa Snow method)
- Encourage rather than take over

**3. Offer choices**
- "Would you like to bathe now or after breakfast?"
- "Which shirt would you like to wear?"
- "Do you prefer a shower or bed bath?"
- Even small choices preserve autonomy

**4. Respect routines and preferences**
- Some people bathe in morning, others at night
- Learn each resident's preferences and honor them
- Don't force your routine on them

**5. Go at the resident's pace**
- Don't rush
- Allow time to process instructions
- Rushing increases anxiety and resistance

**6. Communicate throughout**
- Explain what you're doing before and during care
- Ask permission ("May I help you with your shirt?")
- Narrate: "I'm going to wash your back now"
- Never perform care in silence - it's dehumanizing

## Bathing and Hygiene

### Frequency and Scheduling

**Full baths/showers:** Typically 1-2 times per week for elderly residents (more if incontinent or sweating)
**Daily hygiene:** Face, hands, underarms, perineal area
**Hair washing:** 1-2 times per week, or per resident preference

**IMPORTANT:** Elderly skin is fragile and dries easily. Daily full baths can damage skin. Adjust frequency to resident needs and preferences.

### Types of Baths

**1. Shower**
- Most independent option
- Resident stands or sits in shower chair
- May need supervision or hands-on assistance

**2. Tub Bath**
- Sitting in tub of water
- Requires ability to transfer in/out safely
- May use tub lift or transfer bench

**3. Bed Bath (Sponge Bath)**
- For residents unable to get to shower/tub
- Washing in bed with basin of water and washcloths
- More time-intensive but preserves dignity

**4. Partial Bath**
- Face, hands, underarms, perineal area
- Can be done at sink or bedside
- Good for "off days" between full baths

### Bathing Safety

**Before bathing:**
- Gather all supplies (soap, shampoo, towels, clean clothes)
- Check water temperature (comfortably warm, not hot - test with elbow or thermometer, 100-110°F)
- Ensure bathroom is warm (elderly get cold easily)
- Place non-slip mat in tub/shower
- Have call button within reach

**During bathing:**
- Never leave resident unattended in tub/shower
- Check water temperature throughout
- Use shower chair if resident can't stand safely
- Assist with hard-to-reach areas (back, feet, legs)
- Watch for signs of fatigue, dizziness, shortness of breath
- Grab bars should be installed and used

**After bathing:**
- Assist with drying thoroughly (especially skin folds)
- Apply lotion to prevent dry skin
- Check skin for redness, wounds, rashes
- Assist with dressing
- Clean bathroom (infection control)

### Bathing Techniques for Residents with Dementia

**Common challenges:**
- Fear of water or bathing
- Not recognizing need for bath
- Feeling cold
- Confusion about process
- Perceiving bath as threat or assault

**Effective approaches:**

**1. Make bathing pleasant, not a chore**
- Warm bathroom
- Soft lighting
- Play favorite music
- Use favorite scented soap
- Make it a spa experience, not a task

**2. Timing matters**
- Choose time when resident is calm and rested
- Don't force if they're agitated
- Try again later or next day

**3. Preserve modesty**
- Keep body covered with towels
- Wash under towels when possible
- Close doors and curtains
- Only expose area being washed

**4. Use hand-under-hand technique**
- Place your hand under resident's hand
- Guide their hand to wash themselves
- They maintain sense of control
- More dignified than doing it "to" them

**5. Step-by-step verbal cues**
- "Let's wash your face" (hand them washcloth)
- "Now your arms" (guide movement)
- Simple, one-step instructions

**6. Consider alternatives if shower is frightening**
- Bed bath may be less threatening
- Washcloth baths while dressed in shower
- Gradual desensitization (sit in bathroom, run water without bathing, then progress)

**7. Distraction and reassurance**
- Talk about pleasant topics
- Sing familiar songs
- Provide comfort items (favorite towel)
- Constant reassurance: "You're doing great. Almost done."

## Dressing

### Supporting Independence

**Levels of assistance:**
1. **Independent** - Resident dresses self completely
2. **Supervision** - You observe for safety, provide verbal cues
3. **Set-up assistance** - You lay out clothes in order, resident dresses self
4. **Hands-on assistance** - You help with difficult parts (buttons, zippers, shoes)
5. **Complete assistance** - You dress the resident entirely

**Always use the LEAST assistance necessary.** If resident can do 80% independently, assist only with the 20% they can't do.

### Dressing Techniques

**Putting on shirt/blouse:**
1. Affected or weaker arm first (if resident has weakness from stroke, etc.)
2. Guide arm through sleeve
3. Pull shirt over head or around back
4. Strong arm through second sleeve
5. Button or zip (resident may be able to do this)

**Taking off shirt:**
- Reverse order: Strong arm out first, then weak arm

**Putting on pants:**
1. Sit on bed or chair (safer than standing)
2. Weak leg in first if one-sided weakness
3. Pull pants up to hips while sitting
4. Stand to pull up completely (assist for balance)
5. Fasten buttons/zipper

**Shoes and socks:**
- Socks on first, then shoes
- Resident may use sock aid (assistive device) or long-handled shoehorn
- Shoes with velcro are easier than laces
- Assist with tying laces if needed
- Ensure shoes fit properly and are non-slip

### Adaptive Clothing and Devices

**Adaptive clothing makes dressing easier:**
- Elastic waistbands (no buttons/zippers)
- Velcro closures instead of buttons
- Front-opening garments
- Pull-on pants
- Slip-on shoes

**Assistive devices:**
- Button hooks
- Zipper pulls
- Long-handled shoehorns
- Sock aids (help pull socks on without bending)
- Reachers (grab items without bending)
- Dressing sticks

### Clothing Choices and Dignity

**Residents should choose what to wear when possible:**
- "Which shirt would you like today - blue or red?"
- Offer 2-3 choices (not overwhelming)
- Honor preferences (even if they want to wear same outfit daily)
- Lay out weather-appropriate options

**Maintain dignity:**
- Choose clothing that fits properly (not too tight or revealing)
- Ensure clothing is clean
- Match colors appropriately
- Help residents look presentable (combed hair, clean face)
- Undergarments should always be worn

**For residents with dementia:**
- May not choose appropriately for weather
- May want to wear same outfit repeatedly (have duplicates if possible)
- May put clothes on wrong (backwards, inside-out) - gently assist
- Don't argue about choices unless safety issue (wearing winter coat in summer heat)

## Toileting and Incontinence Care

### Supporting Toileting Independence

**Scheduled toileting:** Take residents to bathroom every 2-3 hours
- After meals
- Before bed
- Upon waking
- Before leaving facility

**Benefits of scheduled toileting:**
- Reduces accidents
- Maintains bladder/bowel function
- Preserves dignity
- Less skin breakdown

### Assisting with Toilet Use

**Steps:**
1. Ask if resident needs to use bathroom (don't assume)
2. Provide privacy - close door
3. Assist with clothing (lowering pants, lifting skirt)
4. Help resident sit safely on toilet
5. **Leave room if resident can safely sit alone** (but stay nearby)
6. Provide call button or leave door slightly open
7. Assist with wiping if needed (front to back for women)
8. Help resident stand and pull up clothing
9. Assist with handwashing
10. Ensure clothing is arranged properly

**Safety considerations:**
- Grab bars should be installed
- Raised toilet seats help those with mobility issues
- Non-slip floor mats
- Good lighting
- Never rush

### Incontinence Management

**Types of incontinence:**
- **Urge incontinence** - Sudden need to urinate, can't make it to bathroom in time
- **Stress incontinence** - Leakage with cough, sneeze, laugh
- **Overflow incontinence** - Bladder doesn't empty completely, constant dribbling
- **Functional incontinence** - Physical/cognitive barriers prevent reaching toilet in time
- **Bowel incontinence** - Inability to control bowel movements

**Your approach:**

**1. Don't shame or embarrass**
- Incontinence is medical, not intentional
- Use matter-of-fact, kind tone
- Never scold, show disgust, or talk about it publicly
- "Let's get you cleaned up and into dry clothes"

**2. Prompt cleanup**
- Change immediately after soiling
- Prolonged contact with urine/feces causes skin breakdown
- Clean thoroughly, dry completely, apply barrier cream

**3. Use proper incontinence products**
- Briefs (not "diapers" - that's infantilizing language)
- Underpads (chux) for bed/chairs
- Proper size and absorbency
- Change regularly even if not fully soiled

**4. Maintain skin integrity**
- Clean with mild soap and warm water (or incontinence wipes)
- Pat dry thoroughly (don't rub)
- Apply moisture barrier cream (zinc oxide, petroleum jelly)
- Check skin for redness, breakdown, rashes
- Turn/reposition bedridden residents every 2 hours

**5. Investigate causes**
- UTIs cause sudden incontinence
- Medications can affect bladder
- Constipation can cause urinary incontinence
- Notify nurse of changes in continence patterns

### Perineal Care (Peri-Care)

**Proper technique is critical for infection prevention:**

**For women:**
- Always wipe/clean **front to back** (prevents bacteria from rectum entering urethra/vagina)
- Separate labia and clean thoroughly
- Use separate areas of washcloth for each wipe
- Rinse thoroughly, dry completely

**For men:**
- If uncircumcised, gently retract foreskin to clean glans
- Clean under foreskin thoroughly
- Return foreskin to normal position (prevents painful swelling)
- Clean scrotum and perineal area
- Rinse thoroughly, dry completely

**Always wear gloves during peri-care and toileting assistance.**

## Grooming

### Hair Care

**Shampooing:**
- 1-2 times per week typically
- Can be done in shower, at sink, or in bed with basin
- Use resident's preferred shampoo
- Rinse thoroughly
- Towel dry, blow dry on low heat if resident tolerates
- Style according to resident preference

**Daily hair brushing:**
- Brush or comb daily
- Detangle gently (start at ends, work up)
- Honor resident's preferred hairstyle
- Men: shaving or beard grooming daily or per preference

**Special considerations:**
- Some residents have cultural/religious practices regarding hair
- Residents with dementia may resist hair care - be gentle, use distraction
- African American hair may need special care products and techniques

### Oral Hygiene

**Critical for health:** Poor oral hygiene leads to gum disease, tooth decay, aspiration pneumonia, and affects nutrition.

**Daily oral care:**
- Brush teeth after each meal and before bed (minimum twice daily)
- Use soft-bristled toothbrush and fluoride toothpaste
- Brush all tooth surfaces, tongue, roof of mouth
- Floss daily if possible
- Rinse thoroughly

**For residents with dentures:**
- Remove dentures after meals
- Brush dentures with denture brush and cleanser
- Rinse mouth and brush gums and tongue
- Soak dentures overnight in denture solution
- Ensure dentures fit properly (loose dentures affect eating and speech)
- Store dentures safely in labeled cup when not worn

**For residents who resist oral care:**
- Use music or distraction
- Hand-under-hand technique (guide their hand to brush their own teeth)
- Break into small steps
- Try different times of day
- Let them hold and chew soft toothbrush (better than nothing)

### Nail Care

**Fingernails:**
- Trim straight across, file smooth edges
- Clean under nails
- Apply lotion to hands and nails
- Check for hangnails, infections

**Toenails:**
- **Important:** In most states, direct care staff can trim fingernails but NOT toenails
- Toenail trimming often requires nurse or podiatrist (risk of injury, especially with diabetes)
- Check your facility policy
- Can wash feet, apply lotion, observe for problems
- Report thickened, ingrown, or fungal nails to nurse

### Shaving

**Men's facial shaving:**
- Electric razor (safer) or safety razor
- If using blade: apply shaving cream, shave in direction of hair growth, rinse, apply aftershave or lotion
- If resident can shave himself, supervise and provide set-up assistance only

**Women may shave legs/underarms:**
- Follow resident preference
- Use safety razor carefully
- Apply shaving cream, rinse thoroughly

**Never shave if:**
- Resident is on blood thinners (risk of bleeding)
- Skin has open wounds or rashes
- Check with nurse first

## Transfers and Mobility Assistance

### Body Mechanics (Protecting Yourself and Residents)

**Proper body mechanics prevent injuries to your back and residents:**

**1. Plan the move**
- Know where you're going
- Clear path of obstacles
- Get help if needed (don't try to move heavy resident alone)
- Use mechanical lifts when appropriate

**2. Use wide base of support**
- Feet shoulder-width apart
- One foot slightly ahead of other
- Provides stability

**3. Bend at knees, not waist**
- Squat down, don't lean over
- Keeps spine straight
- Use leg muscles (strongest muscles) not back

**4. Keep load close to body**
- Hold resident close to your center of gravity
- Don't reach or lean

**5. Avoid twisting**
- Turn with feet, not by twisting spine
- Pivot whole body

**6. Use smooth motions**
- No sudden jerking or pulling
- Count to coordinate: "On three: one, two, three"
- Communicate with resident and other staff

**7. Get help when needed**
- Don't be a hero - ask for two-person assist
- Use mechanical lifts for heavy residents or total dependence
- Facility should have "safe lifting" policy

### Types of Transfers

**1. Stand-Pivot Transfer (partial weight bearing)**
- Resident can bear some weight and stand briefly
- Used for bed to wheelchair, wheelchair to toilet, etc.

**Technique:**
1. Position wheelchair at 45-degree angle to bed/toilet, lock wheels
2. Resident sits at edge of bed/chair, feet flat on floor
3. Stand in front of resident, feet apart, knees bent
4. Resident places hands on your shoulders or on armrests
5. You place hands around resident's back or use gait belt
6. Rock slightly: "One, two, three, stand"
7. Resident stands, you pivot together
8. Resident sits in wheelchair/toilet
9. Ensure positioned safely before releasing

**Use gait belt for safety** - it's a sturdy belt around resident's waist that you hold instead of grabbing clothing or body.

**2. Slide Board Transfer**
- For residents who can sit but can't bear weight to stand
- Smooth board slides resident from bed to wheelchair

**3. Two-Person Transfer**
- For larger or completely dependent residents
- Two staff coordinate to move resident safely

**4. Mechanical Lift (Hoyer Lift)**
- For total dependence or very heavy residents
- Requires training on specific equipment
- Sling slides under resident, attached to lift, lifts and transfers
- Safer for resident and staff

### Ambulation (Walking) Assistance

**Levels of assistance:**
1. **Independent** - Walks alone safely
2. **Supervision** - You walk nearby in case needed
3. **Contact guard** - You walk beside with hand on gait belt, ready to assist
4. **One-person assist** - You provide physical support
5. **Two-person assist** - Two staff needed for safety

**Using assistive devices:**

**Cane:**
- Used on strong side
- Move cane forward, then weak leg, then strong leg
- Provides balance and support

**Walker:**
- Move walker forward, step into it with weak leg, then strong leg
- Ensure rubber tips are intact (slip prevention)
- Adjust height properly (handles at wrist level when standing upright)

**Wheelchair:**
- Always lock wheels before transfers
- Keep footrests up or swung away during transfers
- Ensure feet are on footrests when pushing (not dragging on floor)
- Never leave resident unattended in unlocked wheelchair on incline

**Ambulation safety:**
- Clear pathways (no cords, rugs, clutter)
- Good lighting
- Non-slip footwear
- Encourage use of handrails
- Go at resident's pace
- Watch for fatigue

## Eating and Nutrition Support

### Levels of Eating Assistance

**1. Independent**
- Eats without any assistance
- May need meal set up in accessible location

**2. Set-up assistance**
- You open containers, cut food, butter bread, pour beverages
- Resident feeds self

**3. Verbal cueing**
- Remind to eat, encourage each bite
- "Take a bite of your chicken, Mrs. Jones"

**4. Hand-over-hand assistance**
- Guide resident's hand to plate, bring fork to mouth
- They participate in feeding themselves

**5. Full feeding assistance**
- You feed resident each bite
- Resident unable to feed self

### Feeding Techniques

**Proper positioning:**
- Upright position (90-degree angle if possible)
- Head slightly forward (chin tuck reduces choking risk)
- Feet flat on floor or footrests
- Never feed while lying flat (choking and aspiration risk)

**During feeding:**
- Sit at or below resident's eye level (don't hover over them)
- Allow resident to see food
- Describe what you're offering: "Here's a bite of mashed potatoes"
- Small bites (teaspoon size)
- Wait for resident to swallow before next bite
- Offer beverages between bites
- Don't rush
- Encourage resident to do as much as possible

**For residents with dementia:**
- Reduce distractions (turn off TV, quiet environment)
- One food at a time if overwhelmed by full plate
- Finger foods allow independence (sandwiches, chicken strips, fruit pieces)
- Use hand-under-hand technique if they can self-feed with guidance
- Colored plate contrasts with table (helps with visual perception)
- Verbal and visual cues: "Take a bite" while demonstrating
- May need to initiate each bite: place spoon in hand, guide to mouth

### Aspiration and Choking Prevention

**Aspiration:** Food or liquid goes into lungs instead of stomach (can cause pneumonia)

**Signs of swallowing problems:**
- Coughing or choking during meals
- Wet or gurgly voice after swallowing
- Food pocketing in cheeks
- Drooling
- Refusing food
- Weight loss

**Notify nurse immediately if you observe these signs.**

**Texture-modified diets (ordered by doctor/speech therapist):**
- **Pureed:** Smooth, pudding-like consistency
- **Mechanical soft:** Ground or minced, moist
- **Soft diet:** Soft textures, no tough or hard foods

**Thickened liquids (ordered for aspiration risk):**
- **Nectar-thick:** Coats spoon, drips off slowly
- **Honey-thick:** Pours slowly from cup
- **Pudding-thick:** Eaten with spoon, doesn't pour

**Follow prescribed diet texture exactly - deviating can cause choking or aspiration.**

**Choking response:**
- If resident can cough, encourage coughing (don't interfere)
- If unable to cough, speak, or breathe: Heimlich maneuver or call 911 immediately
- Become certified in Heimlich/CPR (your facility should provide this training)

### Adaptive Equipment for Eating

- **Plate guards:** Prevent food from sliding off plate
- **Non-slip mats:** Keep plate from sliding on table
- **Built-up utensils:** Thicker handles easier to grip
- **Weighted utensils:** Help with tremors
- **Angled utensils:** Easier angle for bringing food to mouth
- **Scoop plates:** High sides for scooping
- **Cups with lids and spouts:** Prevent spills

## Special Considerations for Dementia Care

### Hand-Under-Hand Technique (Teepa Snow)

This technique is revolutionary for personal care with dementia:

**Traditional approach:** "I'm going to wash you now" (doing TO the person)

**Hand-under-hand approach:** You place your hand underneath theirs, guide their hand to perform the task

**Benefits:**
- Person maintains sense of control
- More dignified
- Reduces resistance
- Engages retained abilities
- Less threatening

**How to use:**
- Washing face: Put washcloth in their hand, your hand under theirs, guide to face
- Brushing teeth: Guide their hand holding toothbrush
- Combing hair: Help them comb their own hair
- Eating: Guide fork from plate to mouth

### When Personal Care is Refused

**Common with dementia - try these approaches:**

**1. Ask why (if they can communicate)**
- May be legitimate reason (water too hot, feeling cold, need to use bathroom first)

**2. Change your approach**
- Different staff member may be accepted
- Different time of day
- Break task into smaller steps
- Use distraction and conversation

**3. Try again later**
- Don't force
- Come back in 30 minutes or an hour
- Sometimes residents are more receptive after calming down

**4. Essential vs. preferred**
- If safety issue (severe soiling), may need to gently proceed while minimizing distress
- If preferred but not essential (full bath), can wait until next day

**5. Involve family/care team**
- Maybe family can provide insight into preferences
- Care plan may need adjustment

## Documentation of Personal Care

**Document:**
- Type of care provided (bath, dressing assistance, toileting)
- Level of assistance required
- Resident's response and participation
- Any refusals
- Skin observations (redness, wounds, bruises)
- Changes in ability or needs
- Any concerns or problems

**Example:** "10/30/25, 9:00 AM - Assisted Mrs. Williams with shower. Required hands-on assistance with washing back and feet. Dressed self with set-up assistance. Small reddened area noted on coccyx - will monitor. Ate 75% of breakfast independently. -J. Smith, DCW"

## Key Takeaways

### Remember These Essential Points:

1. **ADLs are the foundation of daily care:** Bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, eating, continence. Your goal is to support independence, not create dependence.

2. **Enable, don't disable:** Let residents do what they can, even if slower or messier. Provide assistance only where needed.

3. **Dignity is paramount in personal care:** Close doors, cover bodies, communicate throughout, never discuss intimate care publicly.

4. **Hand-under-hand technique** (Teepa Snow) is transformative for dementia care - guide their hand to do tasks themselves.

5. **Proper body mechanics prevent injuries:** Wide base, bend knees, keep load close, avoid twisting, get help when needed.

6. **Toileting schedule prevents accidents:** Every 2-3 hours, after meals, before bed, upon waking.

7. **Incontinence is medical, not intentional:** Never shame. Clean promptly, use barrier cream, check skin integrity.

8. **Aspiration risk is serious:** Upright positioning, small bites, watch for coughing/choking, follow prescribed diet textures.

9. **Offer choices to promote autonomy:** "Which shirt?" "Bath now or later?" Even small choices preserve dignity.

10. **Document care provided, participation level, and any changes** in ability or skin condition.
