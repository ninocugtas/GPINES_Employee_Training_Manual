## Why Documentation Matters

### The Legal and Professional Importance

**Documentation is not just paperwork—it's a legal record, a communication tool, and evidence of care provided.** In Adult Foster Care homes, documentation serves multiple critical purposes:

**1. Legal Protection**
- Provides evidence of care provided in case of disputes or litigation
- Demonstrates compliance with Michigan licensing regulations
- Creates a defensible record if questioned by LARA, families, or courts
- Protects both residents and staff when done correctly

**2. Communication and Continuity of Care**
- Ensures all staff members know what happened on previous shifts
- Communicates important changes in resident condition
- Coordinates care among direct care staff, nurses, physicians, and families
- Prevents gaps in care or duplicated efforts

**3. Regulatory Compliance**
- Required by Michigan Adult Foster Care licensing rules
- Subject to review during LARA inspections
- Failure to maintain proper documentation can result in citations, fines, or license revocation

**4. Quality Improvement**
- Tracks patterns and trends in resident health and behavior
- Identifies what interventions work or don't work
- Supports evidence-based care planning
- Demonstrates resident progress or decline

### The Reality: "If It Wasn't Documented, It Didn't Happen"

This phrase is commonly used in healthcare and legal settings because documentation serves as the official record of what occurred. Even if you provided excellent care, **if you didn't document it, you cannot prove it happened.**

**Real-world example:**
A family member claims their mother wasn't given her medication on Tuesday morning. If you gave the medication but didn't document it, there's no proof. The missing documentation creates doubt and potential liability. Proper medication administration records (MAR) protect both the resident and you.

## Michigan Regulatory Framework for Documentation

### Key Michigan Regulations

**Michigan Administrative Code R 400.14301 - Resident Records**
Adult Foster Care facilities must maintain a complete record for each resident containing:
- Resident's name, address, and telephone number
- Emergency contact information
- Physician's name and contact information
- Medical information and care needs
- Medication administration records
- Service plan and updates
- Incident and accident reports
- All required assessments

**Michigan Administrative Code R 400.14207 - Reporting Requirements**
Facilities must report certain events to LARA and other agencies, including:
- Deaths of residents
- Serious injuries requiring emergency medical treatment
- Suspected abuse, neglect, or exploitation
- Communicable diseases
- Fire or natural disasters affecting the facility

**MCL 400.11b - Mandatory Reporting (Act 519 of 2012)**
Direct care staff are **mandatory reporters** and must report suspected abuse, neglect, or exploitation of vulnerable adults to Adult Protective Services within 24 hours.

### Document Retention Requirements

Michigan regulations require AFC facilities to maintain resident records for **at least 6 years** after discharge or death of the resident. Some facilities keep records longer. Never destroy documents without supervisor approval.

## Types of Documentation in AFC Homes

### 1. Admission Documentation

**Required at move-in:**
- Admission agreement and contracts
- Initial health assessment (completed by physician within 30 days)
- Service plan (individualized care plan)
- Resident rights acknowledgment (signed)
- Medication list and physician orders
- Emergency contact information
- Advance directives (if applicable)
- Personal property inventory
- Pre-admission assessment results

### 2. Daily Logs and Shift Reports

**Purpose:** Document routine care, observations, and significant events each shift.

**What to include:**
- Date, time, and shift
- Your name/initials
- Routine care provided (meals, bathing, activities)
- Resident behaviors or mood changes
- Visitors
- Unusual events or concerns
- Follow-up on previous shift concerns

**Example of good daily log entry:**
"10/29/25, 2:00 PM - Mrs. Johnson ate 75% of lunch, participated in afternoon music activity with good engagement. Stated she enjoyed singing hymns. No complaints of pain. Daughter Sarah visited 3:00-4:30 PM. - J.Smith, DCW"

### 3. Medication Administration Records (MAR)

**Purpose:** Document all medications given to residents, as ordered by physicians.

**Critical elements:**
- Resident name and date of birth
- Medication name, dose, route, and frequency
- Time medication was administered
- Staff initials (with corresponding signature page)
- Any refusals or missed doses with explanation
- Any adverse reactions or side effects

**Important:** Medication administration requires specific training. Never administer medications until you've completed medication training and have been authorized by your supervisor.

**Example MAR entry:**
- Medication: Donepezil 10mg PO daily at 8:00 AM
- Date: 10/29/25
- Time given: 8:15 AM
- Initials: JS
- Notes: Taken with breakfast without difficulty

**If medication refused:**
- Circle the time or mark "R" for refused
- Document reason in notes: "Resident stated 'I don't want it today.' Supervisor notified. Will reattempt with PM snack. -JS"

### 4. Incident and Accident Reports

**Purpose:** Document any event that is unusual, harmful, or potentially harmful to residents.

**When to complete an incident report:**
- Falls (even if no apparent injury)
- Injuries of any kind
- Medication errors
- Behavioral incidents (aggression, elopement attempts)
- Resident-to-resident altercations
- Accidents (spills, equipment malfunction)
- Missing personal items
- Complaints from residents or families
- Unexpected hospitalization or emergency room visits

**Critical timing:** Incident reports must be completed **immediately** or as soon as safely possible after the incident. Michigan regulations require reporting serious incidents to LARA within **24 hours**.

**What to include:**
- Date, time, and location of incident
- Resident(s) involved
- Detailed description of what happened (objective facts)
- Witnesses present
- Actions taken (first aid, medical attention, notifications)
- Resident's condition after the incident
- Follow-up required
- Your signature and supervisor's review signature

### 5. Behavior Tracking Logs

**Purpose:** For residents with challenging behaviors (common in dementia care), track patterns to identify triggers and evaluate interventions.

**What to track:**
- Date and time of behavior
- Antecedent (what happened before the behavior)
- Behavior (specific description)
- Consequence (what happened after, how you responded)
- Effectiveness of intervention

**Example:**
- Date/Time: 10/29/25, 7:45 PM
- Antecedent: Evening care routine, staff approached from behind while resident was watching TV
- Behavior: Mr. Davis became agitated, raised voice, pushed staff member's hand away
- Consequence: Staff backed away, gave 5-minute break, approached from front with verbal cue. Mr. Davis calmed, accepted care.
- Intervention effectiveness: Successful - approach from front, not behind

### 6. Communication Logs

**Purpose:** Document communications with family members, physicians, case managers, and other professionals.

**Include:**
- Date and time
- Who you communicated with
- Method (phone, in-person, email)
- Topic discussed
- Any decisions made or follow-up required

**Example:**
"10/29/25, 2:30 PM - Phone call with Mrs. Chen's daughter Lisa (734-555-1234). Discussed mother's decreased appetite over past 3 days. Daughter will discuss with physician at appointment 11/2. Asked to be notified if appetite does not improve by Friday. -JS"

### 7. Service Plan Updates and Reviews

**Purpose:** Service plans (care plans) document individualized care goals and approaches. They must be reviewed and updated regularly.

**Michigan requirement:** Service plans must be reviewed **at least annually** and whenever there's a significant change in the resident's condition.

**Direct care staff role:**
- Provide input on what's working and what's not
- Report changes in resident abilities or needs
- Suggest modifications to care approaches
- Participate in care plan meetings when asked

## Mandatory Reporting Requirements

### What Must Be Reported and When

As a direct care worker in Michigan, you are a **mandatory reporter** under state law. This means you are legally required to report certain situations.

### 1. Suspected Abuse, Neglect, or Exploitation

**Legal requirement:** MCL 400.11b (Mandatory Reporting - Act 519 of 2012)

**Who to report to:** Adult Protective Services (APS)
**Phone:** 855-444-3911 (24/7)
**Timeframe:** Within 24 hours of suspicion

**What constitutes reportable situations:**

**Abuse:**
- Physical harm or injury inflicted intentionally
- Sexual contact or abuse
- Emotional or verbal abuse causing distress
- Financial exploitation or theft

**Neglect:**
- Failure to provide necessary care, supervision, or services
- Withholding food, water, medication, or assistance
- Leaving resident in unsafe conditions
- Ignoring calls for help or care needs

**Important:** You do NOT need proof. You must report if you **suspect** or have **reasonable cause to believe** abuse, neglect, or exploitation has occurred.

**Internal reporting:** ALSO immediately notify your supervisor. Reporting to APS does not replace internal reporting procedures.

### 2. Deaths

**Report to:**
- Michigan LARA (within 24 hours)
- Resident's physician
- Resident's family/emergency contacts
- Hospice (if enrolled)
- Medical examiner (if death is unexpected or unexplained)

**Documentation required:**
- Time and date of death
- Who was present
- Condition leading to death (if known)
- Notifications made and to whom
- Physician contacted and pronouncement of death

### 3. Serious Injuries or Medical Emergencies

**Report to LARA within 24 hours if:**
- Resident requires emergency medical treatment or hospitalization
- Fractures, head injuries, or serious wounds occur
- Unexpected medical crisis (stroke, heart attack, severe allergic reaction)

**Documentation required:**
- Description of injury or emergency
- Circumstances leading to the event
- Medical response (911 called, hospital transport)
- Current condition of resident
- Family notification

### 4. Medication Errors

**Report to:**
- Supervisor immediately
- Resident's physician (supervisor will typically handle this)
- Family (per facility policy)

**Types of medication errors:**
- Wrong medication given
- Wrong dose given
- Medication given to wrong resident
- Medication given at wrong time (significantly early or late)
- Missed dose
- Wrong route (e.g., oral instead of topical)

**Critical documentation:**
- Exactly what happened
- What medication and dose involved
- Time error discovered
- Resident's current condition
- Actions taken (physician notified, resident monitored)
- Plan to prevent future errors

**Important:** Never hide a medication error. Report immediately. Early intervention can prevent serious harm.

### 5. Communicable Diseases

**Report to:**
- Michigan Department of Health and Human Services (MDHHS)
- Local health department
- LARA

**Reportable diseases include:**
- COVID-19
- Influenza outbreaks (multiple residents)
- Norovirus or gastrointestinal outbreaks
- Tuberculosis
- MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus)
- Scabies or lice infestations
- Foodborne illnesses

**Why this matters:** Communicable disease outbreaks in group living settings can spread rapidly. Early reporting allows public health interventions to protect residents.

### 6. Elopement or Missing Residents

**Immediate actions:**
- Search the facility and grounds
- Notify supervisor and all available staff
- Contact family
- Call 911 if resident is not located within 15-30 minutes (per facility policy)

**Report to:**
- Local police immediately if resident has dementia or other condition making them vulnerable
- LARA (within 24 hours)

**Documentation:**
- Time resident was last seen
- When and how absence was discovered
- Search efforts undertaken
- Law enforcement involvement
- Resolution (when resident was located, condition upon return)

## Documentation Best Practices

### The Five C's of Quality Documentation

**1. CLEAR**
- Use simple, straightforward language
- Avoid jargon or abbreviations that others may not understand
- Write legibly (if handwritten)
- Be specific, not vague

**Poor example:** "Resident did okay today."
**Good example:** "Mrs. Johnson ate 80% of breakfast and lunch, participated in morning exercise, conversed pleasantly with staff and other residents. No complaints."

**2. COMPLETE**
- Include all relevant information
- Answer who, what, when, where, and how
- Don't leave gaps that create questions
- Include follow-up actions

**3. CONCISE**
- Include necessary details without rambling
- Stick to relevant facts
- Avoid unnecessary repetition
- Be efficient with words while remaining thorough

**4. CHRONOLOGICAL**
- Record events in the order they occurred
- Include times when relevant
- Show progression of situations

**5. CONFIDENTIAL**
- Protect resident privacy
- Store documents securely
- Never discuss resident information in public areas
- Follow HIPAA and facility confidentiality policies

### Objective vs. Subjective Documentation

**Objective documentation:** Observable facts that can be seen, heard, measured, or counted.

**Examples:**
- "Blood pressure 138/84"
- "Ate 50% of lunch"
- "Walked 10 steps with walker"
- "Stated 'My back hurts'"
- "Crying, hands clenched"

**Subjective documentation:** Your interpretations, opinions, or assumptions.

**Examples to AVOID:**
- "Resident is lazy" (judgment)
- "Resident is faking pain" (assumption)
- "Resident is manipulative" (interpretation)
- "Family doesn't care" (opinion)

**How to document subjective observations objectively:**

❌ **Avoid:** "Mrs. Davis was angry and uncooperative."
✅ **Better:** "Mrs. Davis refused morning care, raised her voice stating 'Leave me alone,' and turned away when staff approached."

❌ **Avoid:** "Mr. Wilson is confused."
✅ **Better:** "Mr. Wilson asked 'Where am I?' four times within 20 minutes and did not recognize his daughter during her visit."

### Use Quotation Marks for Direct Statements

When documenting what a resident said, use their exact words in quotation marks.

**Example:**
"Mrs. Chen stated, 'I have a terrible headache and feel dizzy.' Vital signs taken: BP 158/92, HR 88. Supervisor notified. Resident resting in chair with cool cloth. Will recheck BP in 30 minutes. -JS"

This shows exactly what the resident reported and your response.

### Documenting Behaviors in Dementia Care

When documenting challenging behaviors, use the **ABC format**:

**A - Antecedent** (What happened before the behavior)
**B - Behavior** (Specific description of what the resident did)
**C - Consequence** (What happened after, your intervention, result)

**Example:**
"A: During evening care, approached Mr. Rodriguez from behind while he was in bathroom.
B: Mr. Rodriguez startled, swung arm backward, struck staff member's shoulder (no injury).
C: Staff member backed away, spoke calmly, 'I'm sorry I startled you. I'm here to help.' Gave 5 minutes, then approached from front with visual cue. Mr. Rodriguez accepted care without further incident. Will ensure front approach for all future care. -JS"

### Timeliness: Document Promptly

**Best practice:** Document as soon as possible after the event, while details are fresh.

**Why timeliness matters:**
- Memory fades quickly; details become less accurate over time
- Delays can create gaps in care if next shift doesn't have information
- Late documentation raises questions about accuracy in legal situations

**Realistic approach:** You won't always be able to document immediately (you may be providing care, managing an emergency, etc.). Document as soon as you can safely do so, even if it's at the end of your shift.

**Never:** Backdate documentation to make it appear you documented earlier than you did. If you're documenting late, note the current time and add "late entry" if your facility requires it.

### Correcting Errors in Documentation

**If you make a mistake in handwritten documentation:**
1. Draw a single line through the error (so it's still readable)
2. Write your initials and date above the correction
3. Write the correct information next to it

**Example:**
~~Resident ate 25%~~ JS 10/29 - Resident ate 75% of lunch

**Never:**
- Use correction fluid or white-out
- Scribble out text so it's unreadable
- Erase or destroy documentation
- Remove pages from records

**In electronic documentation systems:**
- Most systems track all changes with timestamps
- Follow your facility's specific procedures for corrections
- Add an addendum or correction note as required

**If you discover you forgot to document something:**
- Add a "late entry" note with current date and time
- Clearly state what date/time the event occurred
- Document what happened

**Example:**
"10/30/25, 8:00 AM - LATE ENTRY regarding 10/29/25, 7:00 PM: Mr. Thompson reported left knee pain (6/10) during evening care. Applied ice pack for 20 minutes, pain reduced to 3/10. Did not request pain medication. Ambulated to bed without difficulty. -JS"

## Confidentiality and HIPAA Compliance

### Understanding Protected Health Information (PHI)

**Protected Health Information (PHI)** includes any information about a resident's:
- Health condition or diagnosis
- Care provided
- Medications
- Mental health status
- Payment for services
- Personal identifiers (name, address, date of birth, Social Security number)

### HIPAA Rules for Direct Care Staff

**Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)** is federal law protecting patient privacy.

**Key rules:**
1. **Access resident information only when necessary for your job**
   - Don't look at records of residents you don't care for
   - Don't access information out of curiosity

2. **Never discuss residents in public areas**
   - No conversations in hallways, elevators, break rooms where others can hear
   - No discussions outside the facility (restaurants, stores, social gatherings)
   - Don't mention residents by name in public

3. **Secure all documents**
   - Don't leave charts, reports, or printouts where unauthorized people can see them
   - Log out of computers when finished
   - Shred documents with PHI before discarding

4. **Don't share information with unauthorized people**
   - Family members must be authorized by the resident (or have legal authority)
   - Don't give information to friends, neighbors, or curious callers
   - Verify identity before sharing information by phone

5. **No social media posts about residents**
   - Never post photos, names, or stories about residents on Facebook, Instagram, etc.
   - Even "anonymous" stories can violate privacy

**Violations can result in:**
- Termination of employment
- Civil penalties ($100-$50,000 per violation)
- Criminal penalties (fines up to $250,000 and prison time for severe violations)

### Who Can Access Resident Information?

**Authorized to receive information:**
- Resident (they have the right to their own information)
- Legal guardian or power of attorney (must verify legal documents)
- Healthcare providers involved in the resident's care
- Designated family members (if resident has authorized, or if they have legal authority)
- LARA inspectors during licensing reviews
- Law enforcement with proper legal documentation (warrant, subpoena)

**Example scenario:**
A woman calls and says, "I'm Mrs. Johnson's niece. How is she doing?"

**Correct response:** "I'm sorry, I cannot share information about residents over the phone without verification. Please speak with our administrator who can verify your authorization and provide updates."

**Don't assume** someone has the right to information, even if they claim to be family.

## Common Documentation Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

### Pitfall #1: Vague or Incomplete Documentation

**Problem:** "Resident had a bad day."
**Issue:** What does "bad day" mean? What happened? What was done?

**Better:** "Mrs. Williams refused breakfast and lunch, ate 30% of dinner. Remained in room most of the day, declined activity participation. Stated 'I just want to be alone today.' No signs of illness. Encouraged fluids throughout day. Will continue to monitor appetite and mood tomorrow. -JS"

### Pitfall #2: Using Judgmental or Inflammatory Language

**Problem:** "Resident was being difficult and manipulative again."
**Issue:** This is opinion/judgment, not objective fact. It's unprofessional and doesn't describe what actually happened.

**Better:** "During morning care, Mr. Lopez refused shower stating 'I don't want to.' When reminded he hadn't bathed in 3 days, he stated 'I'll do it later.' Offered choice of shower or bed bath. Accepted bed bath at 11:00 AM. -JS"

### Pitfall #3: Copying Previous Entries

**Problem:** Repeating the same documentation day after day without observing the resident.
**Issue:** This is fraudulent documentation and can be detected during investigations.

**Solution:** Document what you actually observe each shift. Even if a resident is stable, there will be variations (what they ate, activities they participated in, visitors, conversations).

### Pitfall #4: Documenting Tasks Before They're Done

**Problem:** Signing off that medication was given before actually giving it, or documenting care before providing it.
**Issue:** If an emergency occurs and you don't get to the task, the documentation is now false. This is fraud.

**Solution:** Always document AFTER care is provided or tasks are completed.

### Pitfall #5: Not Documenting Changes or Concerns

**Problem:** Noticing a resident seems "off" but not documenting it because it's not a clear problem.
**Issue:** Subtle changes are early warning signs. If not documented, patterns can't be identified.

**Better:** "Mrs. Anderson ate full breakfast but seemed more quiet than usual. Did not initiate conversation as she typically does. No complaints of pain or discomfort. Alert and oriented. Will continue to monitor. -JS"

### Pitfall #6: Blaming or Criticizing Other Staff in Documentation

**Problem:** "Day shift didn't give Mr. Smith his medication again."
**Issue:** Documentation is not the place for staff conflicts or blame.

**Better:** "Mr. Smith's 9:00 AM medication not documented on MAR. Contacted supervisor. Physician office called to verify if dose should be given late. Per Dr. Lee, dose given at 3:00 PM. Incident report completed. -JS"

## Case Studies: Documentation Scenarios

### Case Study 1: The Fall

**Scenario:**
You're working the evening shift. At 7:30 PM, you hear a loud noise from the hallway. You find Mrs. Patterson on the floor near the bathroom. She says, "I just lost my balance." She's alert and talking. You see a small bruise forming on her left hip.

**What you must do:**
1. **Ensure resident safety** - Don't move her until you assess for injury
2. **Call for help** - Get supervisor or another staff member
3. **Assess resident** - Check for pain, ability to move, visible injuries
4. **Call 911 if serious injury suspected** (severe pain, deformity, head injury, loss of consciousness)
5. **Assist resident** - If no serious injury, help her up (with 2 staff if possible), assist to chair
6. **Monitor** - Check vital signs, observe for changes
7. **Notify** - Supervisor, family, physician (as directed)
8. **Document everything immediately**

**Proper incident report documentation:**

"10/29/25, 7:30 PM - Loud noise heard from hallway. Found Mrs. Patterson sitting on floor in front of bathroom. Resident stated 'I just lost my balance.' Alert and oriented x3, speaking clearly. Small bruise (approx 1 inch diameter) noted on left hip. No other visible injuries. Denied head injury, no loss of consciousness.

Two staff (J. Smith and M. Williams) assisted resident to stand. Resident able to bear weight, walked 10 steps to chair without difficulty. Vital signs: BP 142/86, HR 78, respirations 18.

Supervisor L. Brown notified 7:35 PM. Physician Dr. Anderson notified 7:40 PM, no orders given, advised to monitor. Daughter Sarah contacted 7:45 PM, informed of fall and current condition.

Resident resting comfortably in chair, no complaints of pain at 8:00 PM. No change in condition. Will monitor q 15 minutes x 1 hour, then q 30 minutes x 2 hours for any changes. Fall risk precautions reinforced.

Incident report completed. -J. Smith, DCW"

**Why this documentation is effective:**
- Chronological and detailed
- Objective observations
- Used resident's exact words in quotes
- Documented all notifications with times
- Described resident's condition and response
- Included follow-up monitoring plan

### Case Study 2: The Medication Error

**Scenario:**
You're giving morning medications. You give Mrs. Lopez her medications at 8:30 AM. At 9:15 AM, you realize you accidentally gave Mrs. Lopez Mrs. Chen's blood pressure medication (which Mrs. Lopez doesn't take).

**What you must do:**
1. **Don't panic, but act quickly**
2. **Check the resident immediately** - Assess how she's feeling, check vital signs
3. **Notify supervisor IMMEDIATELY**
4. **Call poison control if directed** (1-800-222-1222)
5. **Contact physician** (supervisor may do this)
6. **Monitor resident closely** - Watch for any adverse effects
7. **Document everything**
8. **Complete incident report**

**DO NOT try to hide the error. Early intervention can prevent serious harm.**

**Proper documentation:**

"10/29/25, 9:15 AM - MEDICATION ERROR DISCOVERED: At 8:30 AM, Mrs. Lopez was mistakenly given Mrs. Chen's Lisinopril 10mg (blood pressure medication) along with her regular medications. Mrs. Lopez is not prescribed this medication.

Error discovered when preparing Mrs. Chen's medications at 9:00 AM. Noticed Lisinopril already removed from package.

Immediately checked Mrs. Lopez - alert, no complaints, vital signs: BP 118/72, HR 76. Resident stated 'I feel fine.'

9:20 AM - Supervisor M. Brown notified
9:25 AM - Dr. Martinez office contacted, nurse advised to monitor BP q 30 minutes x 4 hours, call back if BP drops below 100/60 or resident has symptoms (dizziness, weakness)
9:30 AM - Daughter Lisa notified by phone

Monitoring:
9:30 AM - BP 116/70, no symptoms
10:00 AM - BP 120/74, no symptoms
10:30 AM - BP 118/68, no symptoms

Resident remained stable throughout monitoring period. Incident report completed. Will implement double-check system for all medication administration going forward. -J. Smith, DCW"

**Why this documentation is effective:**
- Immediately disclosed the error
- Described exactly what happened
- Documented resident's condition
- Listed all notifications and actions taken
- Showed close monitoring
- Included plan to prevent future errors

**Remember:** Medication errors happen. Honesty and prompt reporting protect the resident and demonstrate professionalism.

### Case Study 3: Suspected Abuse

**Scenario:**
Mrs. Anderson has dementia and limited verbal communication. During morning care, you notice bruising on both of her upper arms that wasn't there yesterday. The bruises look like finger marks. When you ask what happened, she says, "He was mad at me."

**What you must do:**
1. **Document your observations objectively**
2. **Report to your supervisor immediately**
3. **Call Adult Protective Services** (855-444-3911) within 24 hours - You are a mandatory reporter
4. **Do NOT investigate yourself** - This is APS's role
5. **Preserve evidence** - Photograph bruises if facility policy allows (with supervisor)
6. **Limit contact** with suspected abuser if staff member or another resident

**Proper documentation:**

"10/29/25, 8:00 AM - During morning care, observed bruising on Mrs. Anderson's upper arms bilaterally. Bruises are oval-shaped, approximately 1-2 inches, dark purple in color, located on inner upper arms. These bruises were not present during evening care on 10/28/25.

When asked 'Mrs. Anderson, what happened to your arms?' resident stated 'He was mad at me.' When asked 'Who was mad?' resident became quiet and did not answer further questions.

Resident otherwise alert, no other injuries noted. No complaints of pain when arms gently touched. Able to move arms freely without difficulty.

8:15 AM - Supervisor L. Brown notified and shown bruising
8:30 AM - Adult Protective Services contacted, report filed (reference #APS-2025-12345)
8:45 AM - Photographs taken of bruising per supervisor direction

Resident appearing comfortable, sitting in living room with other residents. Will continue to monitor. -J. Smith, DCW"

**Why this documentation is effective:**
- Detailed physical description of injuries
- Used resident's exact words
- Did not speculate about cause
- Documented all reporting steps with times
- Objective and factual

**Important:** As a mandatory reporter, you can report directly to APS even if your supervisor doesn't want you to. Your legal obligation is to report suspected abuse.

## Special Considerations for Dementia Care Documentation

### Documenting Behaviors

Residents with dementia often exhibit behaviors that are challenging or concerning. Proper documentation helps identify triggers and evaluate interventions.

**What to document:**
- **Trigger/antecedent** (what happened before)
- **Specific behavior** (what the resident did)
- **Your intervention** (how you responded)
- **Outcome** (what happened after your intervention)
- **Time of day** (some behaviors are time-related - "sundowning")
- **Pattern recognition** (is this new or ongoing?)

**Example:**
"10/29/25, 6:45 PM - Mr. Davis became agitated during dinner. Stood up from table, repeatedly asked 'Where's my wife? I need to go home now.' Pacing hallway, wringing hands.

Intervention: Used validation approach. 'You miss your wife. Tell me about her.' Mr. Davis talked about his wife for 5 minutes, showed him photo album. Offered warm tea. Mr. Davis calmed, smiled while looking at photos.

7:00 PM - Agitation resolved. Mr. Davis returned to dining room, ate dessert, engaged in conversation with staff. No further incidents evening shift. Note: This is 3rd occurrence of similar agitation 6:30-7:00 PM this week - possible sundowning pattern. Will discuss care plan adjustment with supervisor. -JS"

### Documenting Decline or Changes

With progressive diseases like Alzheimer's, you'll observe gradual decline. Documenting changes helps families understand progression and supports care planning.

**Examples of changes to document:**
- Increased confusion or disorientation
- Decreased ability to perform ADLs independently
- Changes in mobility or walking
- Increased sleep or decreased activity
- Changes in appetite or eating ability
- New behaviors (wandering, aggression, anxiety)
- Loss of communication ability

**Example:**
"10/29/25 - Over past 2 weeks, observed Mrs. Williams requires increased assistance with dressing. Previously able to dress herself with verbal cues. Now requires hand-over-hand assistance to button shirt and pull up pants. Still able to choose clothes when given 2 options. Discussed with supervisor - will update service plan to reflect increased care needs. -JS"

## Technology and Electronic Documentation

### Electronic Health Records (EHR)

Many AFC facilities now use electronic documentation systems. While the medium is different, the principles remain the same.

**Benefits of electronic documentation:**
- Legibility (no handwriting issues)
- Searchability (can find information quickly)
- Automatic timestamps
- Built-in reminders and alerts
- Easier to share with authorized users

**Challenges:**
- Learning curve with new systems
- Computer/internet issues can delay documentation
- Need for strong password security

**Best practices for electronic documentation:**
1. **Never share your login credentials** - Each staff member must have unique login
2. **Log out when finished** - Don't leave systems open where others can access
3. **Use strong passwords** - Change regularly
4. **Save frequently** - Don't lose work due to technical issues
5. **Still document promptly** - Electronic systems don't change the need for timely documentation

## Quality Assurance and Documentation Audits

### Why Facilities Review Documentation

Your supervisor and facility administrators regularly review documentation to ensure:
- Compliance with regulations
- Quality of care
- Accuracy and completeness
- Identification of patterns or concerns
- Preparation for LARA inspections

**What happens during a documentation audit:**
- Random selection of records
- Review for completeness (all required elements present)
- Review for accuracy (does documentation reflect care provided?)
- Review for timeliness (documented when it should be)
- Identification of patterns (good or concerning)
- Feedback to staff

**If issues are found:**
- You'll receive feedback and re-training
- Patterns of poor documentation may result in disciplinary action
- Serious issues (falsification) can result in termination and legal consequences

**View audits positively:** They're an opportunity to improve your skills and ensure residents receive documented quality care.

## Key Takeaways

### Remember These Essential Points:

1. **Documentation is a legal record** - "If it wasn't documented, it didn't happen." Your documentation can protect or implicate you in legal situations.

2. **You are a mandatory reporter** - Report suspected abuse, neglect, or exploitation to APS (855-444-3911) within 24 hours. This is your legal obligation.

3. **The Five C's** - Clear, Complete, Concise, Chronological, Confidential

4. **Be objective, not judgmental** - Document observable facts, not opinions or interpretations. Use quotes for resident statements.

5. **Document promptly** - As soon as safely possible after events occur. Never backdate or falsify documentation.

6. **Protect resident privacy** - Follow HIPAA rules. Don't discuss residents in public, secure all documents, verify authorization before sharing information.

7. **Report incidents immediately** - Falls, injuries, medication errors, behavioral incidents, elopement. Complete incident reports right away.

8. **Never hide errors** - Report medication errors, documentation mistakes, or concerns immediately. Honesty protects residents and demonstrates professionalism.

9. **Specific Michigan requirements** - Report deaths, serious injuries, suspected abuse, communicable diseases to LARA within 24 hours.

10. **Confidentiality violations have serious consequences** - HIPAA violations can result in termination, fines up to $50,000, and even criminal penalties.
