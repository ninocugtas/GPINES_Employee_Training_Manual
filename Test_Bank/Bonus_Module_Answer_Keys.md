## Version A - Answer Key

**1. What does the acronym F.A.S.T. help you remember for recognizing a stroke?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call 911**

**Explanation:** F.A.S.T. is a critical acronym for recognizing stroke symptoms: **F**ace drooping (ask person to smile - does one side droop?), **A**rm weakness (ask to raise both arms - does one drift down?), **S**peech difficulty (ask to repeat simple phrase - is speech slurred?), **T**ime to call 911 (if ANY of these signs present, call immediately and note time symptoms started). Stroke is time-critical - "time is brain." Treatment must begin within hours for best outcomes. Every minute counts.

---

**2. A resident is clutching their throat, cannot speak, and is turning blue. What should you do FIRST?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Perform abdominal thrusts (Heimlich maneuver)**

**Explanation:** These are signs of severe choking with complete airway obstruction - a life-threatening emergency. Cannot cough, speak, or breathe = severe choking. Perform abdominal thrusts (Heimlich maneuver) immediately: stand behind person, wrap arms around waist, make fist above navel, grasp fist with other hand, give quick upward thrusts. Continue until object is dislodged or person becomes unconscious. Do NOT give water (option A) - they can't swallow with obstructed airway. Don't lay flat (option B) - makes it worse. Gentle back pats (option D) are ineffective for severe choking. This requires immediate action - seconds count.

---

**3. Signs of a heart attack include all of the following EXCEPT:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Sudden, severe headache with face drooping**

**Explanation:** Sudden severe headache with face drooping are signs of STROKE, not heart attack. Heart attack signs include: chest pain/pressure (may feel like indigestion or heartburn), pain spreading to shoulders/arms/jaw/neck/back, shortness of breath, nausea/vomiting, cold sweat, lightheadedness. Women may have different symptoms - less chest pain, more nausea, shortness of breath, jaw/back pain, fatigue. BOTH heart attack and stroke require calling 911 immediately, but treatments are different (aspirin for heart attack, NO aspirin for stroke until CT scan rules out bleeding).

---

**4. A resident with diabetes is confused, sweating, and shaking. You suspect low blood sugar (hypoglycemia). If they are conscious and can swallow, what should you give them?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Fast-acting sugar like juice or regular soda**

**Explanation:** These are classic signs of low blood sugar (hypoglycemia) - a medical emergency that can cause seizures, unconsciousness, brain damage, or death within minutes. If conscious and able to swallow, give fast-acting sugar IMMEDIATELY: 4 oz juice or regular soda (not diet - option A), 3-4 glucose tablets, or 1 tablespoon sugar/honey. Wait 15 minutes, recheck. If improved, give longer-acting carb (crackers, bread). NEVER give insulin (option B) - that lowers blood sugar more. The rule: when in doubt with diabetic emergency, give sugar if conscious. Low blood sugar kills quickly; high blood sugar takes time.

---

**5. When should you ALWAYS call 911 immediately?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) For any situation involving no breathing, no pulse, chest pain, severe bleeding, unresponsiveness, or suspected stroke**

**Explanation:** Call 911 immediately for life-threatening emergencies: no breathing, no pulse, chest pain, severe bleeding that won't stop, unresponsiveness/unconsciousness, choking (can't cough/speak), suspected stroke (FAST), suspected heart attack, seizure >5 minutes or repeated, severe allergic reaction, poisoning, severe burns, suspected fracture with deformity. Don't wait for resident to ask (option A) - they may be unable to. Don't delay for business hours (option C) - emergencies don't wait. Don't try to handle life-threatening situations yourself (option D) - call professionals. When in doubt, call 911 - better to call and not need than delay and have tragedy.

---

**6. A resident has a minor cut on their arm that won't stop bleeding after 10 minutes of pressure. What should you do?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Continue applying pressure and seek medical attention**

**Explanation:** If bleeding hasn't stopped after 10 minutes of continuous direct pressure, continue pressure and seek medical attention (may need stitches, cauterization, or underlying condition like blood thinners needs addressing). Do NOT remove blood-soaked cloth (option A) - this disrupts clot formation; instead add more cloths on top and maintain pressure. Don't pour alcohol directly on wounds (option C) - causes pain and tissue damage. Don't give up (option D) - severe bleeding requires medical intervention. Residents on blood thinners (common in elderly) may bleed longer and more severely from minor injuries.

---

**7. If a resident falls and you suspect they may have a fracture (broken bone), you should:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Call 911, do NOT move them unless in danger, and support the injured area in the position found**

**Explanation:** Fracture signs: deformity, severe pain, swelling, inability to move or bear weight, bone protruding. Call 911 for medical evaluation and transport. Do NOT move person unless in immediate danger (fire, etc.). Moving someone with fracture can cause further damage - especially spine, neck, or hip fractures. Support/stabilize injured area in position found (don't try to straighten). Apply ice if available. Never pull on arms to help stand (option D) - could cause severe injury. Never test if they can walk (option A) - weight-bearing on fracture causes damage. Hip fractures are very common in elderly and absolutely require 911 - never try to move them.

---

**8. What is the correct position for feeding or giving a conscious resident something to drink?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Upright at 90-degree angle**

**Explanation:** Proper positioning prevents choking and aspiration (food/liquid going into lungs). Upright at 90-degree angle with head slightly forward (chin tuck) is safest. NEVER feed lying flat (option A) - dramatically increases choking/aspiration risk and can cause aspiration pneumonia or death. Side-lying (option C) is recovery position for unconscious but breathing person, NOT for feeding. Position absolutely matters (option D is wrong) - it's critical for safety. Even bedbound residents should be elevated to maximum upright position during meals and for 30 minutes after.

---

**9. Which of the following is a sign of shock?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: a) Cool, clammy, pale skin with rapid, weak pulse**

**Explanation:** Shock occurs when organs/tissues don't get enough blood flow (from severe bleeding, heart attack, severe allergic reaction, trauma, dehydration). Signs: cool/clammy/pale skin, rapid weak pulse, rapid shallow breathing, weakness, confusion, anxiety, thirst, nausea, blue lips/fingernails, decreased responsiveness. High fever with hot flushed skin (option B) suggests infection, not shock. Loud strong breathing (option C) is not shock. Pink warm skin with slow pulse (option D) is normal. Treat shock by calling 911, treating underlying cause, laying person down with legs elevated (unless head/neck injury or breathing difficulty), keeping warm, and NOT giving food/drink.

---

**10. Why must you wear gloves when providing first aid that involves contact with blood or body fluids?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) To protect yourself from bloodborne pathogens (diseases spread through blood)**

**Explanation:** Universal precautions require treating ALL blood and body fluids as potentially infectious. Bloodborne pathogens include HIV, Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and others that can be transmitted through blood contact - especially if you have any cuts or breaks in your skin. ALWAYS wear gloves when contact with blood/body fluids is possible. This isn't just about keeping hands clean (option A). Gloves are required whether you have cuts or not (option C is wrong) - microabrasions you can't see can allow transmission. Gloves are NOT optional (option D) - they're required by OSHA and infection control standards. Also wash hands thoroughly before and after, even when gloves worn.

## Version B - Answer Key

**1. The emergency action steps CHECK-CALL-CARE mean:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Check the scene and person, Call 911, Care for the person**

**Explanation:** CHECK-CALL-CARE is the standard emergency response sequence: (1) **CHECK** the scene for safety and check the person (is scene safe to approach? What happened? Is person responsive?), (2) **CALL** 911 or designate someone to call (provide clear information: address, nature of emergency, number injured), (3) **CARE** for the person (provide first aid/CPR as trained, comfort and reassure, monitor until EMS arrives). This systematic approach ensures scene safety, gets professional help coming, and provides care. The other options describe procedures not emergency response steps.

---

**2. A resident complains of chest pain, is short of breath, and sweating. You should:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Call 911 immediately, have them rest, and give aspirin if available and not allergic**

**Explanation:** These are classic heart attack symptoms. Call 911 IMMEDIATELY - every minute matters. Have person stop activity and rest (sitting or lying comfortably). Give aspirin if available and person not allergic: have them chew and swallow 1 regular aspirin (325mg) or 4 baby aspirin (81mg each) - aspirin helps prevent blood clots from enlarging. Loosen tight clothing. Monitor - be ready for CPR. Never give antacids and wait (option A) - this could be fatal delay. Don't wait 30 minutes (option C) - time is critical. Don't have them walk around (option D) - rest is essential; activity increases heart's oxygen demand.

---

**3. During a diabetic emergency, if you're unsure whether a conscious resident has high or low blood sugar, the rule is:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) When in doubt, give sugar - low blood sugar is immediately dangerous**

**Explanation:** **Golden rule for diabetic emergencies:** When in doubt with a CONSCIOUS person who can swallow, give sugar. Why? Low blood sugar (hypoglycemia) can cause seizures, unconsciousness, brain damage, or death within MINUTES - it's immediately life-threatening. High blood sugar (hyperglycemia) takes hours to days to become dangerous. If you give sugar to someone with high blood sugar, it won't help but won't cause immediate harm. If you DON'T give sugar to someone with low blood sugar, they can die. Never give insulin (option C) without knowing blood sugar level. Don't just wait (option D) - low blood sugar is a medical emergency. If unconscious, don't give anything by mouth - call 911.

---

**4. A resident is having a seizure with jerking movements. You should:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Protect them from injury by moving objects away, cushion their head, turn them on their side if possible**

**Explanation:** During a seizure, your goals are: (1) Protect from injury (move furniture/sharp objects away, cushion head with something soft), (2) Do NOT restrain person (option A) - you can't stop seizure and may cause injury, (3) Do NOT put anything in mouth (option B) - that's a myth; you can't "swallow your tongue" and objects in mouth can break teeth or cause choking, (4) Turn on side if possible (prevents choking on saliva), (5) Time the seizure, (6) Stay with person. Don't pour water on them (option D). After seizure: check breathing, place in recovery position, comfort and reassure, don't give food/drink until fully alert. Call 911 if seizure >5 minutes, repeated seizures, injury during seizure, or first-time seizure.

---

**5. Call 911 immediately if a seizure:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: a) Lasts more than 5 minutes or the person has repeated seizures**

**Explanation:** Most seizures last 1-3 minutes and stop on their own. Call 911 if: (1) Seizure lasts MORE than 5 minutes, (2) Person has repeated seizures without regaining consciousness between them, (3) Person is injured during seizure, (4) Seizure happens in water, (5) Person is pregnant or diabetic, (6) This is person's first seizure, (7) Person doesn't regain consciousness after seizure stops. Don't call for EVERY seizure (option C) - some residents have known seizure disorders and brief seizures don't require 911 unless criteria above met. One minute (option B) is too soon - most seizures last 1-3 minutes. Don't wait for permanent breathing cessation (option D) - that's too late.

---

**6. For a minor burn, you should:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: d) Hold under cool running water for 10-20 minutes, then cover with clean bandage**

**Explanation:** Minor burn treatment: (1) Cool immediately with cool (not ice cold) running water for 10-20 minutes or apply cool wet compresses, (2) Cover with sterile non-stick bandage, (3) Give pain reliever if needed. Do NOT use ice directly (option C) - causes more tissue damage. Do NOT apply butter, oil, petroleum jelly, or home remedies (option A) - traps heat and increases damage. Do NOT pop blisters (option B) - increases infection risk. Call 911 or seek medical care for: third-degree burns, second-degree burns larger than 2-3 inches, burns on face/hands/feet/genitals/joints, electrical or chemical burns, or burns in elderly.

---

**7. What is the recovery position and when is it used?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Lying on their side - used for unconscious but breathing person**

**Explanation:** Recovery position is lying on their side (specifically: on side, head tilted back to keep airway open, top leg bent for stability). Used for unconscious but BREATHING person - prevents choking on vomit or tongue blocking airway. NOT for feeding (option A - that's upright). NOT for CPR (option C - that's flat on back on firm surface). NOT for standing (option D). Don't use recovery position if you suspect spinal injury (keep person still) or if not breathing (use CPR position - flat on back). Recovery position is also used after seizure while person is recovering consciousness.

---

**8. Signs of severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) include:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Swelling of face/lips/throat, difficulty breathing, hives, dizziness**

**Explanation:** Anaphylaxis is life-threatening allergic reaction. Signs: **Skin** (hives, itching, flushing, swelling especially face/lips/tongue/throat), **Breathing** (difficulty breathing, wheezing, tight throat, coughing), **Circulation** (dizziness, fainting, rapid pulse, drop in blood pressure), **Stomach** (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, cramping), anxiety/sense of doom. Mild itching and redness (option A) is minor allergy, not anaphylaxis. Sneezing and watery eyes (option C) are allergies but not life-threatening. Mild stomach upset (option D) is minor. Call 911 immediately for anaphylaxis. Use EpiPen if available. Have person lie down with legs elevated (unless breathing difficulty - then sitting better). May need second EpiPen dose if no improvement in 5-15 minutes.

---

**9. If a resident has a nosebleed, you should have them:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Sit upright and lean slightly forward while pinching the soft part of the nose**

**Explanation:** Proper nosebleed treatment: (1) Sit upright and lean SLIGHTLY FORWARD (not back - option B is wrong; tilting back causes blood to go down throat, can cause choking or nausea), (2) Pinch soft part of nose (just below bony bridge) firmly for 10 minutes continuously, (3) Don't release to check before 10 minutes (resets timer), (4) After 10 minutes release pressure, if still bleeding pinch another 10 minutes. Don't lie flat (option A) - same problem as tilting back. Don't pack tissues deep (option D) - can damage tissue. Seek medical attention if bleeding won't stop after 20-30 minutes, very heavy bleeding, on blood thinners, or resulted from injury.

---

**10. When providing first aid, "universal precautions" means:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Treating all blood and body fluids as potentially infectious and always wearing gloves**

**Explanation:** Universal precautions means treating ALL blood and body fluids as potentially infectious, regardless of whether you know the person has a disease. Always wear gloves when contact with blood/body fluids possible. Don't wait to know if person has disease (option A) - you may not know and bloodborne pathogens can be transmitted before symptoms appear. Universal precautions apply EVERYWHERE including AFC homes (option C is wrong) - not just hospitals. Handwashing alone is not enough (option D) - gloves are required protection. Also use barrier devices for rescue breathing, wear eye protection if splashing possible, clean spills with 10% bleach solution, dispose of contaminated materials in biohazard bags, report exposure incidents.

## Version C - Answer Key

**1. A resident suddenly collapses, is unresponsive, and not breathing normally. What should you do FIRST?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Call 911 (or send someone to call) and begin CPR if you're certified**

**Explanation:** This is cardiac arrest - the heart has stopped beating effectively. Call 911 immediately (or send someone while you start CPR) and begin CPR if you're certified. Every minute without CPR decreases survival by 7-10%. Don't spend time checking pulse (option A) - if unresponsive and not breathing, start CPR. Don't give water (option C) - person is unconscious and not breathing. Don't wait 5 minutes (option D) - brain damage begins within 4-6 minutes without oxygen. CPR basics: place on firm flat surface, 100-120 chest compressions per minute at least 2 inches deep, if trained give 2 breaths after every 30 compressions. Get AED if available. Continue until EMS arrives or person starts breathing.

---

**2. What is the Heimlich maneuver (abdominal thrusts) used for?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Clearing an airway obstruction when someone is choking and can't cough or speak**

**Explanation:** Heimlich maneuver (abdominal thrusts) is used for severe choking when person CANNOT cough, speak, or breathe - complete airway obstruction. Stand behind person, wrap arms around waist, make fist just above navel, grasp with other hand, give quick upward thrusts. Repeat until object dislodges or person becomes unconscious. Not for fainting (option A), heart attack (option C), or low blood sugar (option D). If person CAN cough forcefully, don't interfere - encourage coughing which is more effective. Only use Heimlich when person cannot cough/speak/breathe. For pregnant or obese: use chest thrusts instead of abdominal. For unconscious choking victim: begin CPR.

---

**3. You notice a resident has one side of their face drooping, can't raise one arm, and speech is slurred. These are signs of:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Stroke - call 911 immediately**

**Explanation:** These are classic F.A.S.T. stroke signs: **F**ace drooping (one side), **A**rm weakness (can't raise one arm), **S**peech difficulty (slurred). **T**ime to call 911 IMMEDIATELY. Stroke occurs when blood flow to brain is interrupted. Time is critical - "time is brain." Note exact time symptoms started or when person was last seen normal - this is critical for treatment decisions. Have person lie down with head/shoulders slightly elevated. Do NOT give food, drink, or medication. Do NOT give aspirin (unlike heart attack) - some strokes involve bleeding and aspirin makes worse. Monitor breathing. Not low blood sugar (option A) - that causes confusion, sweating, shaking. Not normal aging (option D) - sudden onset of neurological symptoms is medical emergency.

---

**4. When treating severe bleeding, you should:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Apply direct pressure with clean cloth and maintain pressure**

**Explanation:** For severe bleeding: (1) Protect yourself - wear gloves, (2) Apply direct pressure with clean cloth/gauze, (3) Press firmly and continuously, (4) Maintain pressure until bleeding stops or EMS arrives, (5) If cloth becomes soaked, ADD MORE ON TOP - don't remove original cloth (option A is wrong) because this disrupts clot formation, (6) Elevate injured area above heart if possible (unless fracture suspected). Don't pour hydrogen peroxide directly (option C) - causes pain and tissue damage; clean after bleeding controlled. Don't apply tourniquet first (option D) - only use as last resort for life-threatening extremity bleeding when direct pressure fails, and requires training. Call 911 if severe bleeding won't stop.

---

**5. If a resident falls and cannot get up, you should:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Assess for injuries first, call 911 if serious injury suspected, use proper technique to assist up if no serious injury**

**Explanation:** After a fall: (1) Do NOT move immediately - assess first, (2) Ask if they're in pain, check for injuries/deformities/bleeding, check mental status, (3) Call 911 if: hit head, possible fracture, back/neck injury, severe pain, loss of consciousness, can't bear weight, (4) If no serious injury: help rest on floor, assess if can safely get up, use proper assist technique (don't pull on arms - option A is wrong), help to nearby chair, monitor for delayed symptoms, (5) ALWAYS notify nurse and document, complete incident report. Never immediately pull them up without assessing (option A). Don't leave them alone (option B). Don't make them stand to test (option D) - weight-bearing on fracture causes damage. Hip fractures common in elderly - never try to move, call 911.

---

**6. Aspirin should be given during a suspected heart attack because it:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Helps prevent blood clots from getting bigger**

**Explanation:** Most heart attacks are caused by blood clot blocking coronary artery. Aspirin helps prevent blood clot from enlarging and may help dissolve it, improving outcomes. During suspected heart attack: have person chew and swallow 1 regular aspirin (325mg) or 4 baby aspirin (81mg each). Chewing works faster than swallowing whole. Only give if conscious, can swallow, and not allergic. Aspirin doesn't stop fast heart rate (option A), doesn't immediately lower blood pressure (option C), and while it may help with pain (option D), that's not the primary reason - the anti-clotting effect is critical. IMPORTANT: Do NOT give aspirin during stroke until medical evaluation rules out bleeding - some strokes are hemorrhagic (bleeding) and aspirin makes bleeding worse.

---

**7. For a resident having a seizure, you should call 911 if:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: a) The seizure lasts more than 5 minutes or they have repeated seizures**

**Explanation:** Call 911 if seizure: (1) Lasts MORE than 5 minutes, (2) Has repeated seizures without regaining consciousness between, (3) Person is injured during seizure, (4) Happens in water, (5) Person is pregnant or diabetic, (6) First-time seizure, (7) Doesn't regain consciousness after seizure. Most seizures last 1-3 minutes and stop on their own. Don't call for every seizure (option B) or seizures lasting just 30 seconds (option C) - many residents have known seizure disorders with brief seizures that don't require 911. Don't wait for permanent breathing cessation (option D) - that's too late; call at 5 minutes even if still breathing. Note: temporary breathing pause during seizure is normal.

---

**8. The proper treatment for a minor first-degree burn is:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Cool with running water for 10-20 minutes, then cover with sterile bandage**

**Explanation:** Minor burn treatment: (1) Cool IMMEDIATELY with cool (not ice cold) running water for 10-20 minutes or cool wet compresses - this stops burning process and reduces pain/swelling, (2) Pat dry gently, (3) Cover with sterile non-stick bandage, (4) Give over-the-counter pain reliever if needed, (5) Do NOT use ice directly (option A) - causes additional tissue damage from frostbite, (6) Do NOT apply butter, oil, petroleum jelly (option B) - traps heat and increases damage, (7) Do NOT pop blisters (option D) - increases infection risk. Seek medical care for: second-degree burns >2-3 inches, third-degree burns, burns on face/hands/feet/genitals, electrical/chemical burns, elderly patients.

---

**9. Why is it important to note the time when stroke symptoms started?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) It's critical for treatment decisions - stroke treatment is time-sensitive**

**Explanation:** Time is CRITICAL in stroke treatment. Clot-busting medication (tPA) can only be given within 3-4.5 hours of symptom onset and is most effective in first hour ("golden hour"). The exact time symptoms started (or when person was last seen normal) determines treatment eligibility. Every minute of stroke, 1.9 million brain cells die. Emergency teams will ask "What time did symptoms start?" as first question. This isn't just for documentation (option A), billing (option C), or unimportant (option D) - it's the MOST critical piece of information for stroke treatment. If you don't know exact time, tell EMS when person was last seen acting normally. Remember F.A.S.T. - the "T" stands for TIME to call 911.

---

**10. If a resident with a known EpiPen (for severe allergies) shows signs of anaphylaxis (severe allergic reaction), you should:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Call 911 immediately and use the EpiPen as directed**

**Explanation:** Anaphylaxis is life-threatening and requires immediate action: (1) Call 911 FIRST, (2) Use EpiPen immediately: remove from case, grasp firmly orange tip down, pull off blue safety release, place orange tip against outer thigh (can go through clothing), push down hard until click, hold 3 seconds, remove and massage area 10 seconds, note time, (3) Have person lie down with legs elevated (unless difficulty breathing - then sitting), (4) Monitor - may need second EpiPen in 5-15 minutes if no improvement. Don't wait to see if worse (option A) - anaphylaxis can rapidly progress to death. Benadryl alone is not enough (option B) - epinephrine is life-saving. You CAN and SHOULD use someone's prescribed EpiPen in emergency (option D is wrong). EpiPens are designed for emergency use by non-medical personnel.

## Key Concepts Students Should Understand

### Life-Threatening Emergencies - Call 911 Immediately:
- No breathing, no pulse - START CPR
- Chest pain - Give aspirin, call 911
- FAST stroke signs - Call 911, note time
- Severe bleeding - Direct pressure, call 911
- Choking (can't cough/speak) - Heimlich maneuver
- Severe allergic reaction - EpiPen, call 911
- Seizure >5 minutes - Call 911

### Diabetic Emergencies:
**Low blood sugar (hypoglycemia) - More dangerous:**
- Confusion, sweating, shaking
- Give fast-acting sugar if conscious
- **Rule: When in doubt, give sugar**

**High blood sugar (hyperglycemia) - Slower onset:**
- Thirst, frequent urination, fatigue
- Call nurse, encourage water

### First Aid Basics:
- **Burns:** Cool water 10-20 minutes, cover with bandage
- **Bleeding:** Direct pressure, maintain pressure, don't remove soaked cloths
- **Falls:** Assess first, call 911 if serious injury, don't pull on arms
- **Nosebleed:** Sit upright, lean forward, pinch nose 10 minutes
- **Seizure:** Protect from injury, don't restrain, don't put anything in mouth

### Universal Precautions:
- ALWAYS wear gloves for blood/body fluids
- Treat all blood as potentially infectious
- Wash hands before and after
- Report exposure incidents

### Important Phone Numbers:
- **Emergency:** 911
- **Poison Control:** 1-800-222-1222

Students MUST complete hands-on CPR/First Aid certification separately!
