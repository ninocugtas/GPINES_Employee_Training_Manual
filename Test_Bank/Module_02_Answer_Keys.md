# Module 10 Answer Keys
## Personal Care Skills & Activities of Daily Living (ADLs)

---

## Version A - Answer Key

**1. The six basic Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) include all of the following EXCEPT:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Meal preparation**

**Explanation:** The six basic ADLs are: (1) Bathing, (2) Dressing, (3) Toileting, (4) Transferring (mobility), (5) Eating, (6) Continence. Meal preparation is an Instrumental Activity of Daily Living (IADL), which is more complex. IADLs also include housekeeping, medication management, money management, and transportation. In AFC homes, you'll primarily assist with the six basic ADLs.

---

**2. What does "enable, don't disable" mean in personal care?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Let residents do what they can for themselves, providing assistance only where needed**

**Explanation:** "Enable, don't disable" means supporting residents to maintain their abilities by letting them do as much as possible themselves, providing help only where needed. When staff do tasks residents can do themselves (even if slower), residents lose skills through disuse - this is called "learned helplessness" or "excess disability." Always use the LEAST assistance necessary. If a resident can do 80% independently, only assist with the 20% they can't do. This preserves abilities, dignity, and independence.

---

**3. When performing perineal care (peri-care) for a female resident, you should always:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Wipe from front to back to prevent infection**

**Explanation:** For female residents, ALWAYS wipe or clean front to back (never back to front). This prevents bacteria from the rectum entering the urethra or vagina, which causes urinary tract infections (UTIs). Use separate areas of washcloth for each wipe, clean thoroughly, rinse, and dry completely. Always wear gloves during peri-care. This is critical infection control practice.

---

**4. The "hand-under-hand" technique (Teepa Snow method) means:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Placing your hand under the resident's hand to guide them in performing tasks themselves**

**Explanation:** Hand-under-hand (Teepa Snow's Positive Approach to Care) is a revolutionary technique for personal care with dementia. Instead of doing care "to" the person, you place your hand UNDER theirs and guide their hand to perform the task themselves. For example: put washcloth in their hand, your hand under theirs, guide to face to wash. Benefits: person maintains sense of control, more dignified, reduces resistance, engages retained abilities, less threatening. Use for washing, brushing teeth, combing hair, eating.

---

**5. Which of the following demonstrates proper body mechanics when transferring a resident?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Keep a wide base of support, bend at knees, keep resident close to your body, avoid twisting**

**Explanation:** Proper body mechanics prevent injuries to your back and the resident: (1) Wide base of support (feet shoulder-width apart, one foot slightly ahead), (2) Bend at knees not waist (squat, use leg muscles), (3) Keep resident close to your center of gravity, (4) Avoid twisting spine (pivot with feet), (5) Use smooth motions, (6) Get help when needed. Never bend at waist, twist spine, or lift alone if resident is heavy. Use gait belts for safety and mechanical lifts when appropriate.

---

**6. A resident with dementia refuses to take a shower and becomes agitated when you approach. What is the BEST response?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Try a different approach: different time, different staff, use hand-under-hand technique, or try a bed bath instead**

**Explanation:** Refusal of personal care is common with dementia. Never force (option A) or give up entirely (option B). Try different approaches: (1) Different time of day when resident is calmer, (2) Different staff member may be accepted, (3) Hand-under-hand technique, (4) Make bathing pleasant (warm room, music, favorite soap), (5) Try bed bath if shower is frightening, (6) Use distraction and conversation, (7) Come back later. Never shame residents (option D). Investigate why they're refusing - may be fear, cold, pain, or approach method. Essential care (severe soiling) may need to proceed gently, but preferred care (full bath) can wait.

---

**7. When feeding a resident who has difficulty swallowing, what is the PROPER positioning to prevent choking?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Upright position at 90-degree angle with head slightly forward**

**Explanation:** Proper feeding position is CRITICAL to prevent choking and aspiration: Upright at 90-degree angle if possible, head slightly forward (chin tuck reduces choking risk), feet flat on floor or footrests. NEVER feed while lying flat (option A) - this dramatically increases choking and aspiration risk. Reclined (option C) also increases risk. Head tilted back (option D) opens airway making aspiration more likely. Sit at or below resident's eye level, small bites, wait for swallowing between bites, offer beverages, don't rush. Follow prescribed diet textures (pureed, mechanical soft, thickened liquids) exactly.

---

**8. Which statement about incontinence care is TRUE?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Incontinence is a medical issue; change residents immediately, clean thoroughly, and never shame them**

**Explanation:** Incontinence is a medical condition, NOT intentional. Never scold or shame residents (option A) - this is emotional abuse. Change immediately after soiling (not once per shift - option C) because prolonged contact with urine/feces causes painful skin breakdown. Clean thoroughly with mild soap and water, pat dry completely, apply moisture barrier cream, check skin for redness or breakdown. Use proper incontinence products (briefs, not "diapers"). Direct care staff absolutely can provide incontinence care (option D is wrong) - it's a basic ADL. Use scheduled toileting every 2-3 hours to reduce accidents. Never discuss incontinence publicly.

---

**9. When assisting a resident with dressing who has weakness on the right side from a stroke, which arm should go into the sleeve FIRST?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) The weak (right) arm first**

**Explanation:** Dressing rule for one-sided weakness (from stroke, injury): **WEAK ARM FIRST when putting on, STRONG ARM FIRST when taking off.** This is easier and safer for the resident. For putting on shirt: weak/affected arm into sleeve first, pull shirt around back or over head, then strong arm into second sleeve. For taking off: reverse - strong arm out first, then weak arm. Same principle for pants: weak leg first when putting on. Many residents with weakness CAN participate in dressing with proper technique and assistance only where needed - don't assume complete dependence (option D is wrong).

---

**10. A gait belt is used for:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Safety during transfers and walking assistance - provides secure grip around resident's waist**

**Explanation:** A gait belt is a sturdy belt placed around the resident's waist that staff hold during transfers and ambulation for safety. It provides a secure grip instead of grabbing clothing (which can rip) or grabbing the resident's body (which can cause pain or injury). Use gait belts for: stand-pivot transfers, walking assistance, and any mobility support. It's NOT for holding up pants (option A), NOT a restraint (option C - that would be illegal), and NOT for measuring (option D). Gait belts dramatically reduce fall risk and injury to both residents and staff. Always use proper technique: wide grip, hand positioning, body mechanics.

## Version B - Answer Key

**1. Which of the following is one of the six basic ADLs?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Bathing**

**Explanation:** Bathing is one of the six basic ADLs, along with dressing, toileting, transferring, eating, and continence. These are the fundamental self-care tasks for daily living. Medication management (option A), housekeeping (option B), and meal preparation (option D) are Instrumental ADLs (IADLs) - more complex tasks. In AFC homes, direct care staff primarily assist with the six basic ADLs.

---

**2. When assisting a resident with bathing, you should:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Close the door and use towels to cover body parts not being washed to preserve dignity**

**Explanation:** Dignity and privacy are paramount during intimate personal care. Always close doors and curtains, use towels or blankets to cover body parts not currently being washed, only expose the area being cleaned at that moment. Never leave bathroom door open (option A) or discuss personal care publicly (option C) - these violate privacy rights. Don't bathe elderly residents daily unless necessary (option D) - elderly skin is fragile and dries easily; 1-2 full baths per week is typically appropriate (more if incontinent or sweating). Daily: face, hands, underarms, perineal area.

---

**3. Mrs. Johnson can button her shirt but it takes 5 minutes. What should you do?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Let her button it herself, even though it's slower, to maintain her ability and independence**

**Explanation:** This is the essence of "enable, don't disable." If you button it for her to save time (option A), within weeks she'll lose the motor skills and confidence to do it herself through disuse - now she's unnecessarily dependent. Let residents do what they can, even if slower or messier. Your schedule should accommodate residents' pace, not vice versa. Don't criticize slowness (option C). Adaptive clothing (option D) might help, but if she CAN button, support that ability. Maintaining independence preserves dignity, self-worth, and delays decline.

---

**4. Why is it important to offer scheduled toileting every 2-3 hours?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) It reduces accidents, maintains function, and preserves dignity**

**Explanation:** Scheduled toileting (every 2-3 hours, after meals, before bed, upon waking) has multiple benefits: (1) Reduces incontinence accidents, (2) Maintains bladder/bowel function through regular use, (3) Preserves dignity by preventing accidents, (4) Reduces skin breakdown from prolonged contact with urine/feces, (5) Supports residents who can't recognize need or communicate it (common in dementia). It's NOT just regulatory compliance (option A) - it's evidence-based best practice. Don't wait for residents to ask (option D) - many don't recognize need or can't communicate it.

---

**5. A resident shows signs of choking during a meal: unable to cough, speak, or breathe. What should you do?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Perform Heimlich maneuver and/or call 911 immediately**

**Explanation:** If resident CANNOT cough, speak, or breathe, this is COMPLETE airway obstruction - a life-threatening emergency. Perform Heimlich maneuver immediately and/or call 911. Do NOT give water (option A) - they can't swallow with obstructed airway and it makes it worse. Don't lay them flat (option B) or just watch (option D) - they can die within minutes. If resident CAN cough forcefully, encourage coughing (don't interfere) - that's partial obstruction and coughing may clear it. But if unable to cough/speak/breathe = complete obstruction = Heimlich/911 NOW. All staff should be CPR/Heimlich certified.

---

**6. When feeding a resident with dementia who is easily distracted, you should:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Reduce distractions: quiet environment, turn off TV, one food at a time if needed**

**Explanation:** Residents with dementia are easily overwhelmed by stimuli. For successful eating: (1) Reduce distractions - quiet environment, turn off TV, limit conversation, (2) One food at a time if full plate is overwhelming, (3) Use colored plate that contrasts with table (helps visual perception), (4) Finger foods allow independence, (5) Hand-under-hand technique if they can self-feed with guidance, (6) Verbal and visual cues: "Take a bite" while demonstrating. Don't feed in busy, noisy areas (option A). Never rush or force (option C). Don't feed while walking (option D) - huge choking risk.

---

**7. What does aspiration mean?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Food or liquid going into the lungs instead of the stomach**

**Explanation:** Aspiration occurs when food or liquid goes into the lungs (through the trachea) instead of the stomach (through the esophagus). This can cause aspiration pneumonia, a serious and potentially fatal infection. Signs of aspiration risk: coughing/choking during meals, wet or gurgly voice after swallowing, food pocketing in cheeks, drooling. Prevent aspiration with: proper upright positioning (90 degrees, chin tuck), small bites, waiting for swallowing between bites, following prescribed diet textures (pureed, mechanical soft, thickened liquids), alertness during meals. Report swallowing problems to nurse immediately.

---

**8. When a resident with dementia resists personal care, which approach is MOST effective?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Try again later, use different approach, different staff member, or hand-under-hand technique**

**Explanation:** Resistance to care is common with dementia - it's not defiance, it's communication of distress. Never force care (option A) - increases agitation and violates rights. Don't give up entirely (option B). Never threaten (option D) - that's emotional abuse. Instead: (1) Try again in 30-60 minutes when calmer, (2) Different staff member may be accepted, (3) Different approach (hand-under-hand, music, distraction), (4) Different time of day, (5) Make care pleasant not threatening, (6) Investigate why (pain, fear, cold, approach). Essential care may need gentle proceeding, but preferred care can wait. Patience and creativity are key.

---

**9. Proper body mechanics to prevent back injury include:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: d) Bending at the knees, keeping a wide base of support, and holding the resident close to your body**

**Explanation:** Proper body mechanics are essential to prevent injury to yourself and residents: (1) Wide base of support (feet shoulder-width apart), (2) Bend at KNEES not waist (squat using leg muscles, not back), (3) Keep resident CLOSE to your center of gravity (don't reach or lean), (4) Avoid twisting spine (pivot with feet), (5) Smooth motions (no jerking), (6) Get help when needed. Options A, B, and C all describe IMPROPER techniques that cause back injuries. Direct care work is physically demanding - using proper body mechanics throughout your career prevents chronic pain and disability.

---

**10. Why should you NEVER leave a resident lying flat while feeding them?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) It significantly increases the risk of choking and aspiration**

**Explanation:** Feeding while lying flat dramatically increases choking and aspiration risk because gravity pulls food/liquid toward the airway instead of down the esophagus. This can cause aspiration pneumonia and death. ALWAYS feed residents in upright position (90-degree angle if possible) with head slightly forward (chin tuck). While discomfort (option A) and visibility (option D) are considerations, the primary reason is SAFETY - choking and aspiration prevention. This is non-negotiable. Even residents in bed should be elevated to maximum upright position during feeding. Keep upright for 30 minutes after eating.

## Version C - Answer Key

**1. What is the primary goal when assisting residents with ADLs?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Support residents in maintaining independence by letting them do what they can themselves**

**Explanation:** The primary goal of ADL assistance is to support independence - "enable, don't disable." Your job is NOT to do everything for residents (option B) or rush through tasks (option A). Let residents do as much as possible themselves, providing assistance only where needed. This maintains their abilities, preserves dignity, promotes autonomy, and delays decline. Use the LEAST assistance necessary. All residents deserve this approach (option D is wrong) - whether they have dementia or not, support their remaining abilities.

---

**2. Which of the following demonstrates preserving dignity during bathing?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Closing doors, using towels to cover the body, and explaining what you're doing throughout**

**Explanation:** Dignity during intimate care requires: (1) Close doors and curtains for privacy, (2) Cover body with towels - only expose area currently being washed, (3) Communicate throughout - explain what you're doing, ask permission, narrate actions, (4) Use respectful, professional tone, (5) Never discuss personal care publicly. Open doors (option A), public discussions (option B), and bathing multiple residents together (option D) all violate privacy and dignity rights. Treat residents as you'd want to be treated during vulnerable moments.

---

**3. When assisting a resident with incontinence, you should:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: a) Change them immediately, clean thoroughly, never shame them - incontinence is medical, not intentional**

**Explanation:** Incontinence is a medical condition, NOT intentional or within resident's control. Your response must be: (1) Change IMMEDIATELY after soiling (not end of shift - option B), (2) Clean thoroughly with mild soap and water, pat dry, apply barrier cream, (3) NEVER shame, scold, or show disgust - use matter-of-fact, kind tone, (4) Never discuss publicly (option D). Telling residents to "try harder" (option C) is shaming and unhelpful - if they could control it, they would. Prolonged contact with urine/feces causes painful skin breakdown. Treat with compassion and professionalism.

---

**4. A resident with dementia keeps putting their clothes on backwards. What should you do?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Gently assist them to put clothes on correctly, use hand-under-hand technique**

**Explanation:** Residents with dementia often have difficulty with sequencing and spatial relationships, leading to backwards/inside-out clothing. Don't scold (option A) - they're not doing it intentionally. Don't take over completely (option B) - that creates unnecessary dependence. Do gently assist: hand-under-hand technique, visual and verbal cues, help them correct it, consider clothing with clear front/back indicators. While sometimes letting it go is fine if not a safety issue (option D), helping them dress appropriately maintains dignity and appearance. Balance support with preserving abilities.

---

**5. When transferring a resident from bed to wheelchair, when should the wheelchair wheels be locked?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) Before the transfer begins - wheels must always be locked during transfers**

**Explanation:** Wheelchair wheels MUST be locked BEFORE any transfer begins (option C is correct). If wheels aren't locked, the wheelchair can roll away during transfer, causing falls and serious injuries to resident and staff. Lock wheels before every transfer, every time, no exceptions. Check that they're actually locked (push wheelchair to verify it doesn't move). After transfer is complete and resident is safely seated, wheels can be unlocked for mobility. Option A (after) and option B (only if asked) are dangerous. This is basic safety protocol.

---

**6. What is the correct water temperature for bathing residents?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Comfortably warm (100-110°F), test with elbow or thermometer**

**Explanation:** Water temperature for bathing should be comfortably warm (100-110°F) - warm enough to be pleasant but not hot enough to burn. Test water temperature with your elbow (like testing baby bottle) or use a bath thermometer before resident enters. Very hot water (option A, 120°F) can burn fragile elderly skin. Cool water (option C) can cause discomfort and hypothermia - elderly get cold easily. Staff preference (option D) doesn't matter - resident comfort and safety matter. Check temperature throughout bath as it can cool. Elderly have decreased ability to sense temperature extremes, increasing burn risk.

---

**7. Signs that a resident may be having swallowing problems include:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Coughing or choking during meals, wet or gurgly voice after swallowing**

**Explanation:** Signs of swallowing problems (dysphagia): (1) Coughing/choking during meals, (2) Wet or gurgly voice after swallowing, (3) Food pocketing in cheeks, (4) Drooling, (5) Refusing food, (6) Weight loss, (7) Taking very long to eat, (8) Frequent throat clearing. These signs indicate aspiration risk - food/liquid going into lungs instead of stomach. Report to nurse immediately if you observe these signs. Resident may need swallowing evaluation by speech therapist and prescribed diet texture modifications (pureed, mechanical soft, thickened liquids). Options A, C, and D describe normal, healthy eating.

---

**8. Which of the following is an example of offering choices to promote autonomy?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: c) "Which shirt would you like to wear today - blue or red?"**

**Explanation:** Offering choices promotes autonomy and preserves dignity. Present 2-3 choices (not overwhelming): "Which shirt?" "Bath now or after breakfast?" "Shower or bed bath?" Even small choices give residents sense of control over their lives. Options A, B, and D are all directive statements that don't offer choice - they tell residents what's happening without input. Even residents with dementia can often make simple choices when presented appropriately. Choices should be meaningful and honored. This is fundamental to person-centered, rights-based care.

---

**9. Why is it dangerous to rush residents during personal care tasks?**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Rushing increases anxiety, resistance, and risk of injury to both resident and staff**

**Explanation:** Rushing residents during care causes multiple problems: (1) Increases anxiety and agitation (especially in dementia), (2) Increases resistance and refusals (feeling pressured), (3) Increases fall risk (resident tries to keep up with rushed pace), (4) Increases injury risk to staff (poor body mechanics when hurried), (5) Decreases quality of care, (6) Violates dignity. Residents absolutely notice when you're rushing (option C is wrong) and it affects them (option D is wrong). Your schedule must accommodate residents' pace, not vice versa. Option A is wrong - care quality matters more than speed. Slow down, be patient, allow time.

---

**10. Adaptive equipment for eating (plate guards, built-up utensils, non-slip mats) helps residents by:**

**CORRECT ANSWER: b) Promoting independence by helping residents feed themselves**

**Explanation:** Adaptive equipment promotes independence by compensating for physical limitations: plate guards prevent food from sliding off, non-slip mats keep plates stable, built-up utensils are easier to grip, weighted utensils help with tremors, angled utensils bring food to mouth more easily. These tools allow residents to feed THEMSELVES who otherwise would need staff feeding assistance. It's NOT primarily for staff convenience (option A). While meeting regulations is good (option C), the real purpose is promoting independence. Equipment supplements staff assistance (option D) but doesn't replace it entirely - some residents still need hands-on help even with adaptive equipment.

## Key Concepts Students Should Understand

If students miss these questions, review the following concepts:

### The Six Basic ADLs
1. Bathing
2. Dressing
3. Toileting
4. Transferring (mobility)
5. Eating
6. Continence

### Enable, Don't Disable
- Let residents do what they can themselves
- Provide assistance only where needed
- Use LEAST assistance necessary
- Maintains abilities, dignity, independence
- Prevents learned helplessness

### Hand-Under-Hand Technique (Teepa Snow)
- Place YOUR hand UNDER resident's hand
- Guide THEIR hand to perform task
- They maintain sense of control
- More dignified than doing "to" them
- Use for washing, brushing teeth, eating, etc.

### Dignity in Personal Care
- Close doors and curtains
- Cover body parts not being washed
- Communicate throughout care
- Never discuss personal care publicly
- Treat with respect during vulnerable moments

### Proper Body Mechanics
- Wide base of support
- Bend at knees, not waist
- Keep resident close to body
- Avoid twisting spine
- Use smooth motions
- Get help when needed
- Use gait belts and mechanical lifts

### Incontinence Care
- Medical condition, not intentional
- Change immediately
- Clean thoroughly, apply barrier cream
- NEVER shame or scold
- Scheduled toileting every 2-3 hours

### Perineal Care (Critical!)
- Females: ALWAYS front to back (prevents UTI)
- Males: If uncircumcised, retract foreskin to clean
- Clean thoroughly, dry completely
- Always wear gloves

### Feeding Safety
- Upright position (90 degrees)
- Head slightly forward (chin tuck)
- NEVER feed lying flat (aspiration risk)
- Small bites, wait for swallowing
- Watch for coughing/choking
- Follow prescribed diet textures

### Aspiration Prevention
- Food/liquid going into lungs
- Can cause aspiration pneumonia (fatal)
- Proper positioning critical
- Watch for signs: coughing, wet voice, pocketing
- Report swallowing problems immediately

### Dressing with One-Sided Weakness
- WEAK ARM FIRST when putting on
- STRONG ARM FIRST when taking off
- Same for pants: weak leg first

### Dementia Care Approaches
- Hand-under-hand technique
- Simple instructions, physical guidance
- Try different times/staff if resistance
- Make care pleasant, not threatening
- Never force or shame
- Patience and creativity
